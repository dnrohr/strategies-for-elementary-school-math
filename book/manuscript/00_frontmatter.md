# How We Think About Arithmetic

## Opening spread

### One answer, many minds

Try this before reading any further:

> **7 + 5 = ?**

Perhaps you counted. Perhaps you saw twelve. Perhaps a ten appeared, with two left over. Perhaps you heard the number, moved fingers, pictured dots, or simply knew.

The answer is the same. The experience may not be.

This book is an illustrated atlas of those differences. Each chapter starts with an elementary problem and follows several routes to the same result. We look at the mathematics of a route—what was grouped, transformed, remembered, or measured—and at its representation—what the solver may have seen, heard, said, felt, or not sensed at all.

These are two questions, not one. A solver can use decomposition while imagining blocks, hearing number words, moving along a number line, or experiencing only an abstract relation. A solver can also change routes from one problem to the next. No page assigns a person a permanent “type.”

The invitation is simple: solve first, then compare. There is no prize for having the most vivid picture or the fastest answer. The point is to notice that mathematical thinking has more than one visible shape.

## Strategy gallery

### How to use this book

1. **Cover the answer.** Solve the chapter’s problem in whatever way arrives first.
2. **Notice the route.** Ask: Did I count, retrieve, split, compensate, compare, share, or make a picture?
3. **Notice the format.** Ask: Did I hear words, see symbols or objects, feel movement, arrange space, use my hands, or experience no particular sensory image?
4. **Read across the gallery.** The accounts are invitations to compare possibilities, not tests of your ability.
5. **Try a second route.** Use a number line, objects, words, a written algorithm, or a deliberate pause before retrieval.

You may find that a method that feels natural for addition feels awkward for fractions. You may also find that the same method feels different when the numbers, setting, or time available changes. The pages use “might” and “could” deliberately: introspection is partial, and a book cannot see every step of a person’s thinking.

### A note on constructed solver voices

Unless a page explicitly identifies a published interview or research report, every first-person voice in this book is a **constructed representative account**. It is written to make a mathematically plausible route vivid. Every method repeats the label **Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**, so this disclosure remains visible even when a strategy page is read apart from the introduction:

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I start at seven and count five more: eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve.”

That sentence is not a quotation from a particular child. It does not establish that a particular age, group, diagnosis, or population thinks this way. The accounts combine an operation with a possible representational format so readers can compare them. They are editorial examples, not evidence.

Research notes identify what studies actually support, what remains mixed, and what is an illustration of possibility. The book does not treat visual, verbal, spatial, motor, or abstract processing as fixed learning styles, and it does not use a strategy to diagnose a person.

### Two axes: the route and the experience

Every strategy card carries two kinds of tags.

**Axis A — Computational strategy: what changed?**

This names the mathematical operation or relationship:

- **Counting:** keep track of individual units or successive jumps.
- **Retrieval:** recognize and recall a known fact or pattern.
- **Decomposition:** split a number into useful parts, such as `7 + 5 = 7 + 3 + 2`.
- **Compensation:** use a nearby easy number, then correct the change, such as `15 − 8 = 15 − 10 + 2`.
- **Relational reasoning:** use an inverse, equivalence, distance, or preserved total.
- **Algorithm:** follow a conventional written sequence, such as regrouping in columns.
- **Geometric or embodied reasoning:** use arrays, areas, objects, fingers, gesture, or movement as part of the route.

**Axis B — Representation: how might it have appeared?**

This names a possible format of the experience:

- **Verbal/auditory:** inner speech, number words, a rhythm, or a remembered phrase.
- **Visual-symbolic:** printed or imagined numerals and written steps.
- **Visual-concrete:** dots, blocks, coins, counters, fingers, or fraction bars.
- **Spatial/geometric:** distances, jumps, regions, rows, or a number line.
- **Motor/embodied:** finger extension, tapping, imagined writing, or gesture.
- **Abstract/non-sensory:** a relation or answer without a describable picture or voice.
- **Mixed:** more than one format at once or in quick succession.

The axes can vary independently. “Decomposition” is not the same as “seeing blocks.” “Visual” does not tell us which calculation happened. When a page shows the same route in several formats, it is making that separation visible.

### Visual legend

The book’s small visual system keeps the two axes readable:

| Mark | Meaning | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| **Constructed solver voice** | An editorial example, never an unattributed participant quotation | “I start at seven…” |
| **Route tag** | The mathematical transformation | `make ten`, `retrieve`, `count on` |
| **Format tag** | A possible representational form | `verbal`, `spatial`, `motor` |
| **Blue arrows** | Forward movement or adding a part | jumps from 7 toward 12 |
| **Coral arrows** | Removal or correction | take away 8; subtract the compensation |
| **Green brackets** | A group or equal partition | three groups of four |
| **Dotted outlines** | An imagined or optional object | a mental row of dots |
| **Solid outlines** | A written or physically present object | counters on a table |
| **Small “same math” badge** | Same computation, alternate format | `10 × 12 + 12` shown as words and an array |
| **Question bubble** | A reader-facing observation prompt | “What appeared first?” |
| **Explanatory diagram caption** | The picture records the mathematics but does not claim to reproduce an inner scene | a sparse equality map for a non-sensory account |

Color is never the only cue: arrows, labels, line style, and grouping carry the meaning too. Precise numerals and mathematical labels belong to the layout layer, not inside generated raster art.

## Cross-classification

Here is the book’s central matrix in miniature. One computation—split eleven into ten and one—can be represented in several ways.

| Computation: `11 × 12 = 10 × 12 + 1 × 12` | Verbal | Visual-symbolic | Concrete | Spatial | Motor | Abstract |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Decompose 11** | “Ten twelves and one twelve.” | `10 × 12 + 1 × 12` appears as written numerals. | Ten rows of 12 objects, plus one row of 12. | A 11-by-12 rectangle is split after row 10. | A hand marks ten groups, then one. | The relation “eleven is ten plus one” is recognized without a sensory image. |

The computation column tells us what is preserved: eleven groups of twelve become ten groups plus one group. The other columns describe possible ways of carrying that relation. These descriptions are not mutually exclusive. A person might see a rectangle while silently saying “ten times twelve,” or use fingers to keep place while writing symbols.

## Research notes

The distinction between conceptual relations and procedures is supported by studies reporting interaction in both directions: conceptual and procedural knowledge can each contribute to later gains, and their relationship is iterative rather than a simple opposition ([R01-007], [R01-008]). Research on arithmetic strategy use also documents multiple strategies and strategic change rather than one universal route ([R01-002], [R01-013]). These findings motivate the book’s framework, but they do not prove that any particular constructed voice describes a real participant.

Evidence about inner speech likewise treats it as heterogeneous in condensation, dialogic quality, function, and sensory detail ([R02-I01]). The representational tags here are descriptive prompts, not psychological categories or diagnoses. The matrix is an editorial tool for separating mathematical operation from possible experience.
