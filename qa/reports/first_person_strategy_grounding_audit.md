# First-person strategy grounding audit

Task ID: `QA-EXPERIENCE-GROUNDING`
Audit date: 2026-09-12
Scope: the 175 method accounts in CH01–CH14

## Question

Can each constructed first-person account be written as a variation on a
mathematical strategy or representational form that has actually been observed
in children, rather than as an unconstrained invention?

## Short answer

Yes at the level of broad strategy families, with several qualifications.
Most of the book's computational routes can be nested under strategies that
have been observed through task-based interviews, concurrent observation,
children's immediate reports, written work, or longitudinal assessment.
Several of the book's representational forms also have direct interview
anchors. However, a source for a family does not authenticate the exact words,
exact problem, or exact combination of strategy and phenomenology used in a
constructed account.

The appropriate editorial relation is:

> observed family -> mathematically exact variation -> constructed voice

It is not:

> source -> paraphrase of a particular participant

No current account should be converted into an empirical quotation unless the
wording is checked against the original source and attributed.

## Evidence standard used in this audit

| Code | Meaning | Permitted manuscript use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `D` | Direct child evidence: observed action, immediate strategy report, interview description, or written solution | A constructed account may vary the documented family while retaining the disclosure. |
| `F` | Family-level evidence: the computational family is documented, but the depicted sensory form is not | Keep the computational route; describe the experience as one possible constructed form. |
| `M` | Mathematical-only grounding: exact transformation or established algorithm, without child-use evidence located in this pass | Retain only if the atlas intentionally includes mathematical possibilities; do not imply typicality. |
| `P` | Provisional source: relevant record found, but full text was not checked in this pass | Do not promote it to canonical manuscript support until verified. |

## The Russian book

The likely book is V. A. Krutetskii's *The Psychology of Mathematical
Abilities in Schoolchildren* (Russian original, 1968; English translation,
1976). This audit checked the searchable full text of the 1998 Russian reprint,
not merely a secondary summary.

Krutetskii is useful because the book reports systematic task observations,
solution records, and student remarks. It directly supports these broad forms:

- **compressed or curtailed reasoning**: intermediate links can become so
  abbreviated that a response appears immediate; the book also cautions that
  an observer may be unable to reconstruct the child's full process
  (Russian reprint, pp. 217–218 and 292–293);
- **flexible switching among solutions**: students were asked for multiple
  solutions and were observed changing operations or abandoning a previous
  route (pp. 298–311);
- **reverse reasoning**: reasoning from a result or transformed state back
  toward the initial data (pp. 312 onward);
- **verbal-logical versus visual-schematic support**: some observed students
  worked readily with abstract schemes and little visual support, while others
  repeatedly sought visual or schematic interpretations (pp. 343–350);
- **search for a simpler representation**: one recorded student rejected a
  cumbersome variable assignment and rebuilt the representation so that the
  problem could be solved mentally (p. 311).

These findings ground the existence of compressed, flexible, reversible,
abstract, and visual-schematic approaches. They do **not** establish fixed
learner types, and they should not be generalized from mathematically able,
mostly older schoolchildren to every elementary learner. Krutetskii himself
used caution when assigning younger participants to types.

Source checked: [Krutetskii, Russian full text](https://djvu.online/file/8LSTZgw42mj5h).
An English-language secondary account lists formalization, generalization,
symbol use, sequential logic, curtailment, reversibility, flexibility,
mathematical memory, and spatial thinking as the nine analyzed abilities
([Chamberlin, 2010](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ906121.pdf), pp. 54–61).

## Direct evidence for subjective arithmetic representations

### Pitta and Gray (1997)

This is the closest source located to the book's central device. Eight- to
twelve-year-olds were given one- and two-digit addition and subtraction items
and asked in semi-structured interviews what came to mind before and during
solution. Reports were supported by field notes and audio/video records.

Documented forms included:

- analogical images of discrete objects such as counters or marbles;
- number-track or number-line-like images;
- imagined actions on counters during count-on or decrementing procedures;
- symbolic numerical images;
- a sequence of multiples associated with a multiplication table;
- rapidly appearing or “flashing” problem and answer symbols;
- reports in which the answer appeared with little reportable intervening
  process.

This supplies `D` grounding for the book's object imagery, number-track
imagery, dynamic counting imagery, symbolic flashes, multiplication-table
sequences, and near-immediate answers. It does not support attaching those
forms to every strategy or child, and its achievement-group interpretation
should not be copied into the book as a hierarchy.

Source checked: [Pitta & Gray, 1997, author-hosted paper](https://www.davidtall.com/eddiegray/97a-cyprus.pdf),
pp. 29–41.

### Heirdsfield and Cooper (2002)

In in-depth interviews with Year 3 children, one child used separation and
whole-number compensation routes such as adding 100 and subtracting 1. Another
consistently reported a **mental image of the pen-and-paper algorithm**—numbers
imagined one under the other and processed by place value. This gives `D`
grounding for mental written layout as an experienced strategy, not merely a
working-memory inference.

The same study's strategy framework distinguishes:

- separation left-to-right or right-to-left;
- aggregation, retaining one number and adding/subtracting chunks of the
  other;
- holistic compensation around a nearby convenient number;
- mental use of the written algorithm;
- estimation, monitoring, and trying an alternative strategy.

Source checked: [Heirdsfield & Cooper, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0732-3123(02)00103-7),
pp. 57–74; author copy checked from the QUT repository.

## Documented computational inventory

### Early addition and subtraction

Directly observed/reported families (`D`):

- model both quantities with objects and count all;
- finger counting and verbal counting;
- count all from one, count from the first/lower addend, and count from the
  larger addend;
- count on and count back;
- retrieval or “just knew” as a report category;
- decomposition/transformation;
- derived facts using identity, commutativity, doubles or nearby facts;
- inverse or missing-addend reasoning;
- visible or imagined number-track movement;
- mental image of a written algorithm.

Primary anchors already verified in the project include R01-003, R01-013, and
R01-014. Additional direct-method evidence comes from the observed/self-report
coding in [Chu et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6312299/)
and the convergent observation/self-report study of second- and third-graders
in [Geary et al., 2007/2013 PMC edition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3607623/).

Two-digit mental addition/subtraction families (`D` or strong `F`):

- separation by place value;
- aggregation or sequential jumps;
- compensation around a friendly number;
- standard written procedure, including its mental image;
- estimation and exact checking;
- more than one valid route for the same problem.

Project anchor R01-010 directly documents jump and compensation with
third-graders. Heirdsfield and Cooper directly document separation,
aggregation, holistic compensation, estimation, and mental written layout.

### Multiplication and division

Directly observed/interview-assessed families (`D`):

- make or share equal groups and count each item by ones;
- rhythmic, double, or skip counting over visible groups;
- count composite units when individual items are hidden;
- repeated addition for multiplication;
- repeated subtraction for division;
- retrieve or derive a multiplication fact;
- use multiplication and division as inverse operations;
- recognize commutativity of equal groups;
- direct modeling with objects or an array;
- move from direct modeling to counting/adding and then to invented
  algorithms using decomposition or newly formed composite units.

The most useful checked source is Mulligan and Wright's videotaped interview
framework for 5–7-year-olds. It explicitly records perceptual unit counting,
rhythmic/double/skip counting, figurative composite units, repeated
addition/subtraction, fact derivation, commutativity, and inverse use
([Mulligan & Wright, 2000](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED452034.pdf),
pp. 4-16–4-24). Project anchor R01-002 independently documents retrieval,
repeated addition, object-set counting, and writing the problem across repeated
multiplication assessments.

For multidigit multiplication, the established child-strategy progression is:

1. direct modeling with ones;
2. direct modeling with tens and ones;
3. counting/adding groups while abstracting the amount in each group;
4. invented algorithms that decompose factors, form groups of groups, or use
   partial products;
5. conventional written algorithms.

The principal reference is Ambrose, Baek, and Carpenter, “Children's invention
of multidigit multiplication and division algorithms,” in *The Development of
Arithmetic Concepts and Skills* (2003), pp. 305–336. A full primary chapter
copy was not located in this pass, so this source is `P` until checked. The
National Academies' research synthesis independently states that children do
invent many multidigit procedures and connects them to decomposition,
diagrams, concrete materials, and conventional algorithms
([Adding It Up](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9822/chapter/8)).

### Fractions

Observed or experimentally elicited families (`D` or strong `F`):

- equal sharing and successive distribution;
- equal partitioning of a whole;
- treating a fraction as a magnitude on a number line;
- area/rectangle and circle models;
- benchmark comparisons, including comparison with one-half;
- common-denominator/equivalent-fraction procedures;
- cross-product or “butterfly” procedures;
- unit-fraction composition;
- correct and incorrect symbolic rules;
- estimation followed by an exact procedure.

Empson's first-grade classroom study directly analyzes children's solutions and
discussion in equal-sharing tasks ([Empson, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1207/S1532690XCI1703_3)).
Project sources R03-005–R03-007 provide experimental evidence for number-line,
measurement, benchmark, and feedback-supported magnitude reasoning. R03-011,
R03-013, and R03-014 document fraction procedures, systematic rules, and
unit-fraction/common-unit reasoning.

Liu and Jacobson analyzed 214 fourth-graders' written fraction-ordering
solutions and identified 12 strategies, including rectangle, circle,
cross-product/butterfly, common-denominator, and number-line approaches. The
publisher record and detailed abstract were inspected, but not a complete
author copy, so this is `P` for canonical ledger purposes
([Liu & Jacobson, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmathb.2022.100985)).

## Accounts requiring the most caution

These accounts can remain, but they should not be presented as if research had
recorded this exact route in children:

- CH05 M12–M15: ×11 pattern, double/halve, factor regrouping, powers of two;
- CH06 M11: proportion table;
- CH08 M09: proportion;
- CH09 M07–M08: complement-to-one and retrieval-plus-check;
- CH10 M08–M10: estimation, fairness check, and material-dependent answer
  format;
- CH11 M06–M07, M13–M14, and M17–M18: near-square, double/halve,
  money-like chunks, lattice, taps, and estimation/refinement;
- CH12 M06 and M08–M10: make-a-thousand, thousand-frame, block piles, and
  abacus-like place map;
- CH13 M09 and CH14 M09–M10: decimal check, denominator inner speech, and
  non-sensory execution.

The concern is not mathematical correctness. It is the strength of the claim
that this is a known child strategy or known experience.

## Recommended editorial protocol

For each method, add three internal fields during the next manuscript revision:

```text
Observed base: [source ID + locator]
Variation type: exact problem substitution / representation change / sequence change
Experience status: directly attested / constructed on an attested family / mathematical-only
```

Apply these rules:

1. Preserve the global constructed-account disclosure.
2. Base every computational route on a documented family or label it
   mathematical-only.
3. Use direct phenomenological sources only for the form they actually report:
   counters do not automatically support blocks, visual symbols do not
   automatically support moving digits, and working-memory activation does not
   support conscious imagery.
4. Do not turn Krutetskii's analytic/geometric descriptions into permanent
   child types. Use them as evidence that verbal-logical and visual-schematic
   routes both occurred in the observed sample.
5. Do not infer frequency from existence. A rare observed strategy is still
   real, but “a solver might” is different from “many children.”
6. Distinguish a child's spontaneous strategy from one elicited, instructed,
   or made available by materials.
7. Keep exact quotations out of the constructed voice unless quotation wording,
   translation, participant context, and permission/copyright constraints are
   checked.
