# 24 ÷ 6 = ?

## Opening spread

The answer is **4**, but division can be experienced in more than one direction. “Share 24 into 6 equal groups” asks for the size of each group. “How many groups of 6 fit in 24?” asks for the number of groups. Both are exact, and both are constructed representative accounts—not quotations from research participants.

Before reading, solve `24 ÷ 6`. Did you distribute, count groups, remember `6 × 4`, walk backward, or simply know?

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I share twenty-four into six groups
**Strategy class:** partitive division / equal sharing
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + motor
**Math:** `24 ÷ 6 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I draw six empty circles. I deal the twenty-four counters around the circles, one at a time, until every counter is used. Each circle has four. I am finding how many each group receives.”
### Steps
1. Make 6 equal groups.
2. Deal 24 objects one per group in repeated rounds.
3. Continue until all objects are placed.
4. Count 4 objects in each group.
### Illustration brief
Vector or composite scene with exactly 24 counters distributed into six outlined bowls, four per bowl. Show four complete dealing rounds; verify no counter is duplicated or left over.
### Mathematical note
This is sharing (partitive) division: the number of groups is known, and the size of each equal group is found.
### Research note
Equal-sharing is a constructed mathematical model. R03-008 is screened and supports only cautious discussion of sharing contexts; it is not evidence for a universal developmental sequence.
### Tags
`division`, `equal-sharing`, `concrete`, `motor`

## Method 02 — I ask how many sixes fit
**Strategy class:** quotitive division / grouping
**Phenomenology:** spatial + verbal
**Math:** `6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24`, so `24 ÷ 6 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I keep taking groups of six from twenty-four. The first six leaves eighteen, then twelve, then six, then zero. I made four groups, so the answer is four. This time I am counting groups, not objects in each group.”
### Steps
1. Start with 24.
2. Remove one group of 6 repeatedly.
3. Record `24, 18, 12, 6, 0`.
4. Count the groups: 4.
### Illustration brief
Horizontal row of 24 counters partitioned into four contiguous groups of six, with four directional brackets. Use a separate “groups counted” marker so the image does not imply sharing.
### Mathematical note
This is grouping (quotitive) division: the group size is known, and the number of groups is found.
### Research note
The partitive/quotitive distinction is mathematical terminology used here as an explanatory lens; no claim is made about which model children prefer.
### Tags
`division`, `grouping`, `repeated-subtraction`, `spatial`

## Method 03 — I remember six times four
**Strategy class:** inverse multiplication / retrieval
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal
**Math:** `6 × 4 = 24`, therefore `24 ÷ 6 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “The division sign makes me look for a multiplication fact. Six times four is twenty-four, so four is the missing factor. I do not need to distribute counters; I recognize the related fact and check it by multiplying back.”
### Steps
1. Rewrite the question as `6 × ? = 24`.
2. Retrieve `6 × 4 = 24`.
3. Fill the missing factor: `? = 4`.
4. Multiply back to verify.
### Illustration brief
Clean vector fact-family triangle linking `6 × 4`, `4 × 6`, and `24 ÷ 6`; keep arrows relational rather than implying a neural mechanism.
### Mathematical note
Division undoes multiplication. The missing factor in `6 × ? = 24` is exactly the quotient.
### Research note
R01-002 is verified and supports cautious framing of overlapping multiplication strategies and retrieval; it does not establish that retrieval is always fastest or available.
### Tags
`inverse`, `retrieval`, `multiplication-facts`, `abstract`

## Method 04 — I skip-count by sixes
**Strategy class:** repeated addition / skip counting
**Phenomenology:** verbal + rhythmic
**Math:** `6, 12, 18, 24` → four counts
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I say six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four in a steady beat. I keep track of how many sixes I have said. The fourth number reaches twenty-four, so the quotient is four. I can record four jumps on a number line to show why the count stops there.”
### Steps
1. Begin at 0.
2. Add 6: 6 (one group).
3. Continue: 12, 18, 24.
4. Count four jumps.
### Illustration brief
Precise number line from 0 to 24 with four equal jumps of 6 and beat markers. Labels are vector text; check endpoint and jump count.
### Mathematical note
Skip-counting is repeated addition used to find how many equal groups reach the dividend.
### Research note
Counting and strategy development are discussed cautiously with R01-001 and R01-003, both screened; the account is constructed, not a participant report.
### Tags
`skip-counting`, `repeated-addition`, `rhythm`, `number-line`

## Method 05 — I subtract six until nothing remains
**Strategy class:** repeated subtraction
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + sequential
**Math:** `24 − 6 − 6 − 6 − 6 = 0`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I write twenty-four and keep crossing off six. After one subtraction I see eighteen; then twelve; then six; then zero. The number of subtractions is my answer: four. I label each subtraction so I do not confuse the amount removed with the number of groups I made.”
### Steps
1. Write 24 and a counter at 0.
2. Subtract 6 to get 18; count 1.
3. Subtract 6 twice more to get 6; count 3.
4. Subtract 6 once more to get 0; count 4.
### Illustration brief
Vector subtraction ladder with exact rows `24 → 18 → 12 → 6 → 0` and four subtraction marks of 6. Do not depict negative values or unequal removals.
### Mathematical note
Repeated subtraction and grouping ask the same quotient question through a sequence of equal removals.
### Research note
R01-006 is screened and R01-007 verified; they motivate separating conceptual and procedural descriptions, not a frequency claim.
### Tags
`repeated-subtraction`, `algorithmic`, `sequential`

## Method 06 — I partition an array into rows
**Strategy class:** array partition / geometric reasoning
**Phenomenology:** geometric + visual-concrete
**Math:** `4 rows × 6 = 24`, so `24 ÷ 6 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I arrange twenty-four dots as four rows of six. The divisor tells me the row length. When I count the rows, I get four. I can turn the rectangle and see the related multiplication fact.”
### Steps
1. Arrange 24 dots in equal rows of 6.
2. Count the rows.
3. Observe `4 × 6 = 24`.
4. State the quotient as 4.
### Illustration brief
Exact 4-by-6 dot array with row braces labeled 6 and a row count labeled 4. Include a rotated miniature only if it remains exact.
### Mathematical note
An array makes the inverse relationship visible: dividend = number of groups × group size.
### Research note
The array is a mathematical representation. R01-002 is verified and supports discussing related multiplication strategy families without treating the picture as evidence of imagery.
### Tags
`array`, `geometry`, `inverse`, `visual-concrete`

## Method 07 — I halve twice
**Strategy class:** factor decomposition
**Phenomenology:** spatial + abstract
**Math:** `24 ÷ 6 = (24 ÷ 2) ÷ 3 = 12 ÷ 3 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Six is two times three. I first split twenty-four into two equal halves, making twelve. Then I split twelve into three equal groups. That leaves four in each group, which is the same as dividing by six.”
### Steps
1. Factor 6 as `2 × 3`.
2. Divide 24 by 2 to get 12.
3. Divide 12 by 3 to get 4.
4. Check `4 × 6 = 24`.
### Illustration brief
Branching decomposition diagram: 24 → two groups of 12 → each 12 partitioned into three groups of 4. Use exact labels and nested braces.
### Mathematical note
Dividing successively by factors 2 and 3 is equivalent to dividing by their product 6.
### Research note
This factor route is a constructed exact transformation. Avoid implying a fixed learner type; R01-002 is verified but concerns strategy overlap in multiplication contexts.
### Tags
`factorization`, `decomposition`, `spatial`

## Method 08 — I use coins and equal piles
**Strategy class:** physical grouping / contextual model
**Phenomenology:** motor + visual-concrete
**Math:** 24 coins in 6 piles of 4
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I pretend the twenty-four is twenty-four coins. I make six piles, giving each pile one coin at a time. Four rounds finish the piles. I can answer four because each pile has four coins.”
### Steps
1. Represent 24 as 24 identical counters.
2. Make 6 labeled piles.
3. Deal one counter to each pile for four rounds.
4. Read the pile size: 4.
### Illustration brief
Composite tabletop scene with six labeled spaces and exactly 24 identical coins, four per pile. Keep labels vector and ensure the dealing sequence is consistent.
### Mathematical note
The context supplies a tangible equal-sharing model; interchangeable units keep the quotient equal to 4.
### Research note
R02-F06 is screened and concerns gesture/counting coordination; cite only as a lead pending full-text confirmation, not as proof that objects are necessary.
### Tags
`physical-sharing`, `motor`, `concrete`, `equal-groups`

## Method 09 — I track four groups on my fingers
**Strategy class:** embodied counting support
**Phenomenology:** motor + verbal
**Math:** four groups of six reach 24
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I count groups of six, lifting one finger for each group: six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four. Four fingers are up when I arrive. My fingers are keeping track of groups, not representing all twenty-four objects.”
### Steps
1. Start with 0 groups and closed fingers.
2. Count by six while raising one finger per group.
3. Raise four fingers at 24.
4. Read the group count as 4.
### Illustration brief
Anatomically correct hand with four raised fingers, paired with a vector number line showing four jumps of six. Do not imply fingers encode a fixed cognitive style.
### Mathematical note
Fingers serve as an external counter for the number of groups; they do not change the division.
### Research note
R02-F05 is verified and concerns finger knowledge/use in a sampled group; it does not establish causation or a universal finger strategy.
### Tags
`fingers`, `embodied`, `skip-counting`, `motor`

## Method 10 — I use a bar model
**Strategy class:** part-whole model
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + spatial
**Math:** a 24-unit bar split into 6 equal parts gives `24 ÷ 6 = 4`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I draw one long bar for twenty-four. I split it into six equal sections. Since the whole bar is 24 and there are six sections, each section must be four. The quotient is the size of one section.”
### Steps
1. Draw a bar labeled 24.
2. Partition it into 6 equal sections.
3. Divide the total length equally.
4. Label every section 4.
### Illustration brief
Exact horizontal bar with six congruent segments, total label 24, per-segment label 4, and a brace marking “6 equal groups.”
### Mathematical note
The bar foregrounds the partitive meaning: six equal parts compose a whole of 24, so each part measures 4.
### Research note
This diagram is a constructed representation. The chapter avoids claiming that one model is inherently superior.
### Tags
`bar-model`, `part-whole`, `spatial`, `symbolic`

## Method 11 — I use a proportion table
**Strategy class:** relational scaling
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + abstract
**Math:** `6 groups → 24 objects`; `1 group → 4 objects`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I make a tiny table. Six groups hold twenty-four objects. One group is one sixth as many, so I divide twenty-four by six. The table says one group holds four, and six groups of four rebuild twenty-four.”
### Steps
1. Write `6 groups : 24 objects`.
2. Scale groups from 6 to 1 by dividing by 6.
3. Divide 24 by 6 to get 4 objects.
4. Rebuild the relationship with `6 × 4 = 24`.
### Illustration brief
Vector two-row table with columns for 6 and 1 groups, and 24 and 4 objects; arrows show division by 6 and reverse multiplication.
### Mathematical note
The quotient preserves the constant group size in a proportional relationship.
### Research note
This is a mathematical relational model; it is not presented as a universal developmental route.
### Tags
`proportion`, `scaling`, `relational`, `symbolic`

## Method 12 — I estimate, then verify by multiplication
**Strategy class:** magnitude check / inverse verification
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal
**Math:** `24 ÷ 6` should be near 4; `4 × 6 = 24`
### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Six goes into twenty-four several times, but not ten times. Four feels plausible because four sixes are the whole. I test it: four times six is exactly twenty-four. The multiplication check turns a guess into an answer.”
### Steps
1. Predict that the quotient is a small whole number.
2. Test `4 × 6`.
3. Confirm it equals 24.
4. State `24 ÷ 6 = 4`.
### Illustration brief
Minimal vector scale showing 24 divided into four equal blocks of 6, with a green verification loop from `4 × 6` back to 24. Mark estimation as a check.
### Mathematical note
Estimation narrows the search; inverse multiplication verifies exact equality.
### Research note
R01-007 is verified and supports keeping conceptual and procedural knowledge distinct. This constructed account makes no claim about strategy frequency.
### Tags
`estimation`, `verification`, `inverse`, `abstract`
