# 15 − 8 = ?

## Opening spread

Fifteen take away eight leaves **7**. But “take away” is only one possible picture. A solver might remove eight things, walk backward, build the distance from 8 to 15, or recognize a remembered fact. The arithmetic can be identical while the experienced scene changes.

> The first-person accounts in this gallery are constructed representative examples. They are not quotations from research participants.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I remove eight counters

**Strategy class:** count-all + physical removal
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + motor
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I see fifteen counters. I slide eight of them away, one at a time, and look at what is still in the group. The seven that stay are my answer. I keep the removed counters in a separate space so the two quantities do not get mixed.”

### Steps

1. Make one group of exactly 15 counters.
2. Move 8 counters out of the group.
3. Count the counters that remain: 7.

### Illustration brief

Show 15 identical counters in a 3-by-5 array; a hand moves exactly 8 (the rightmost column of 5 plus 3 from the next column) into a separate tray. The remaining tray visibly contains 7. Use vector overlays for `15 − 8 = 7`; no raster text.

### Mathematical note

Subtraction is modeled as removing a subset. The original 15 is partitioned into 8 removed and 7 remaining, so `15 = 8 + 7`.

### Research note

This is a constructed representation of a take-away model, not evidence that a particular solver uses counters. Strategy repertoires can overlap and change by problem [R01-002, verified].

### Tags

`take-away`, `counting`, `visual-concrete`, `motor`

## Method 02 — I count backward eight beats

**Strategy class:** count-back
**Phenomenology:** verbal/auditory + rhythmic motor
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I start at fifteen and say the numbers backward: fourteen, thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven. I keep eight beats, and the last number I say is seven. My starting number stays visible while I mark each backward step, so I can check that I stepped exactly eight times.”

### Steps

1. Start at 15; do not count 15 as a subtraction step.
2. Say 14, 13, 12, 11, 10, 9, 8, 7—eight backward moves.
3. Stop at 7.

### Illustration brief

Use a vector number line from 7 through 15 with eight left-pointing unit arrows: `15→14→13→12→11→10→9→8→7`. Add eight beat marks; the start is 15 and endpoint 7.

### Mathematical note

Each backward move decreases the quantity by one. Eight such moves decrease 15 by 8, giving `15 − 8 = 7`.

### Research note

Counting is one strategy in an overlapping repertoire; evidence does not support treating it as a fixed stage or inherently deficient method [R01-002, verified; R01-003, verified].

### Tags

`count-back`, `number-words`, `rhythm`, `number-line`

## Method 03 — I count up from eight to fifteen

**Strategy class:** relational reasoning + count-on
**Phenomenology:** spatial + verbal
**Math:** `8 + 2 + 5 = 15`, so `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I treat the question as ‘What do I add to eight to reach fifteen?’ I jump from eight to ten—two—and then from ten to fifteen—five. Two plus five is seven, so the gap is seven.”

### Steps

1. Start at 8 and count upward to 10: `10 − 8 = 2`.
2. Count from 10 to 15: `15 − 10 = 5`.
3. Add the distances: `2 + 5 = 7`.

### Illustration brief

Draw a vector number line with labeled 8, 10, and 15. Show a 2-unit jump 8→10 and a 5-unit jump 10→15; label combined distance 7. Do not show a backward removal arrow.

### Mathematical note

The difference between 8 and 15 is the sum of adjacent gaps: `(10 − 8) + (15 − 10) = 2 + 5 = 7`.

### Research note

“Difference as distance” is an exact model; the source record supports varied strategy repertoires, not this private imagery for any individual [R01-002, verified].

### Tags

`difference`, `count-on`, `make-ten`, `spatial`, `verbal`

## Method 04 — I subtract five, then three

**Strategy class:** decomposition
**Phenomenology:** inner speech + abstract relational
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 15 − 5 − 3 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Eight is five and three. Fifteen minus five is ten, and ten minus three is seven. I hear the short chain in my head, with ten as the middle stop. Splitting the eight lets me use the friendly ten without changing the total amount removed.”

### Steps

1. Decompose 8 into `5 + 3`.
2. Calculate `15 − 5 = 10`.
3. Calculate `10 − 3 = 7`.

### Illustration brief

Show `8 = 5 + 3`, then a ten-frame labeled 10 with 3 counters removed to leave 7. The two removal stages total exactly 8.

### Mathematical note

`15 − (5 + 3) = (15 − 5) − 3`; subtracting a sum can be done in stages.

### Research note

Decomposition and counting may coexist, and strategy choice depends on task and solver; do not infer a fixed developmental type [R01-002, verified; R01-013, verified].

### Tags

`decomposition`, `ten-anchor`, `inner-speech`, `ten`

## Method 05 — I subtract ten, then put two back

**Strategy class:** compensation
**Phenomenology:** symbolic + relational
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 15 − 10 + 2 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Ten is easier to subtract than eight. I take away ten and reach five, but that took away two too many, so I add those two back. Five plus two is seven.”

### Steps

1. Replace 8 with the nearby benchmark 10.
2. Compute `15 − 10 = 5`.
3. Restore the 2 extra units: `5 + 2 = 7`.

### Illustration brief

Use a vector diagram: subtract-10 from 15 ends at 5; a return arrow of 2 ends at 7. State `−10 + 2 = −8`; quantities must not imply the answer is 5.

### Mathematical note

Subtracting 10 and adding 2 has net effect subtracting 8: `−10 + 2 = −8`.

### Research note

Compensation can be executed accurately, but possession and adaptive selection differ [R01-010, verified]. The account is constructed.

### Tags

`compensation`, `benchmark`, `ten`, `relational`

## Method 06 — I ask what plus eight makes fifteen

**Strategy class:** inverse operation
**Phenomenology:** verbal equation + abstract relation
**Math:** `8 + 7 = 15`, therefore `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “The subtraction sign turns into a missing-addend question: eight plus what equals fifteen? I know eight plus seven is fifteen, so the missing part is seven. I can place eight and seven together on a bar that reaches fifteen, then read the unlabeled part as the answer.”

### Steps

1. Rewrite as `8 + □ = 15`.
2. Recall or derive `8 + 7 = 15`.
3. Fill the box with 7.

### Illustration brief

Show `15 − 8 = □` changing to `8 + □ = 15`, with a seven-unit bar filling the blank. Keep inverse arrows exact.

### Mathematical note

Addition and subtraction are inverse operations: if `8 + 7 = 15`, removing 8 from 15 leaves 7.

### Research note

Understanding additive relations is associated with inverse and decomposition strategies, but not a simple causal developmental sequence [R01-005, screened; R01-014, verified].

### Tags

`inverse`, `missing-addend`, `relational`, `verbal`

## Method 07 — I measure the gap

**Strategy class:** difference-as-distance
**Phenomenology:** spatial + visual-symbolic
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I do not picture eight objects disappearing. I picture 8 and 15 as two marks on a line and look at the space between them. The space has seven unit lengths.”

### Steps

1. Place 8 and 15 on one number line.
2. Partition 8→10 (2 units) and 10→15 (5 units).
3. Combine the intervals: `2 + 5 = 7`.

### Illustration brief

Make the gap central: endpoint markers at 8 and 15, a bracket spanning 7 units, ticks at 9–14, and a split at 10 labeled 2 and 5. No removal imagery.

### Mathematical note

Distance from 8 to 15 is `15 − 8`, whether traversed forward or imagined as a static interval.

### Research note

Number-line descriptions are representational possibilities, not proof of a universal mental number line. Strategy studies do not measure private imagery directly [R01-002, verified; R01 caveat].

### Tags

`difference`, `distance`, `number-line`, `visual-spatial`

## Method 08 — I use my fingers to keep track

**Strategy class:** embodied counting + count-back
**Phenomenology:** motor + visual-concrete
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I hold fifteen in mind and bend one finger for each backward count. After eight bends I have tracked eight steps, and seven is where the count lands. My fingers are a record of the steps, not a picture of seven fingers.”

### Steps

1. Start the count at 15.
2. Bend or tap one finger for each of eight backward steps: 14 through 7.
3. Read the endpoint as 7.

### Illustration brief

Show one hand making eight sequential tracking marks across a strip; do not depict eight fingers on one hand. Pair with a number line ending at 7. A second inset may show 15 counters and exactly 8 marked steps.

### Mathematical note

The fingers externally track eight decrements; they do not change the arithmetic transformation.

### Research note

Finger use, finger gnosis, gesture, and finger-related activation are distinct constructs. Reviews report associations and unsettled causal mechanisms [R02-F01, verified; R02-F02, verified]. This account is constructed.

### Tags

`fingers`, `embodied`, `tracking`, `count-back`

## Method 09 — I see a bar with eight covered

**Strategy class:** part-whole / bar model
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + spatial
**Math:** `15 = 8 + 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “A bar of fifteen squares is whole. I shade the first eight squares as the part I know, and the unshaded segment is the part I need. I count that uncovered segment: seven.”

### Steps

1. Draw a bar partitioned into 15 equal unit squares.
2. Shade exactly 8 squares.
3. Count the unshaded 7 squares as the difference.

### Illustration brief

Use one 15-unit bar with an 8-unit shaded segment and 7-unit unshaded segment. Label whole 15 and parts 8 and 7; avoid a removal arrow so it reads as difference.

### Mathematical note

The bar encodes `8 + 7 = 15`; solving for the unknown part gives `15 − 8 = 7`.

### Research note

This is an editorial visual model, not a claim that bar models are naturally preferred. Coordinating representations can support understanding, but evidence is task- and curriculum-specific [R01-007, verified].

### Tags

`bar-model`, `part-whole`, `visual`, `difference`

## Method 10 — I split fifteen into ten and five

**Strategy class:** decomposition + make-ten anchor
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + spatial
**Math:** `15 − 8 = (10 − 8) + 5 = 2 + 5 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I see fifteen as ten and five. Eight takes two from the ten, leaving two, and the five sits beside it. Two and five make seven. The exchange makes the subtraction visible: I first make ten available, then combine the leftover two with the untouched five.”

### Steps

1. Decompose `15 = 10 + 5`.
2. Calculate `10 − 8 = 2`.
3. Combine with the untouched 5: `2 + 5 = 7`.

### Illustration brief

Show a ten-frame containing 10 and a separate group of 5. Remove 8 from the ten-frame, leaving 2; place those 2 beside 5 to form 7. Label `(10 − 8) + 5`.

### Mathematical note

Because 8 is contained in the ten-part, `15 − 8 = (10 + 5) − 8 = (10 − 8) + 5`.

### Research note

Ten-based decomposition is a valid constructed strategy. Evidence supports overlapping repertoires, while the particular inner image is editorial [R01-002, verified; R01-003, verified].

### Tags

`make-ten`, `decomposition`, `ten-frame`, `visual-symbolic`

## Method 11 — Seven appears as a remembered fact

**Strategy class:** retrieval
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal or visual-symbolic (variable)
**Math:** `15 − 8 = 7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I read fifteen minus eight and seven arrives without a visible sequence. Sometimes it has the sound of a number word; sometimes it is simply the relation between the symbols. I can still check it by thinking eight plus seven.”

### Steps

1. Encode the operands 15 and 8.
2. Access the answer relation `15 − 8 = 7`.
3. Optionally verify with `8 + 7 = 15`.

### Illustration brief

Keep the central image quiet: `15 − 8` resolves into a vector `7` card, with optional verification loop `7 + 8 = 15`. Do not add invented speed claims or a literal brain-memory picture.

### Mathematical note

The result is the same equality as derived methods; the difference is whether the solver experiences access without consciously reported intermediate steps.

### Research note

Research often infers retrieval from response patterns, which do not provide transparent access to private experience. Retrieval and derived procedures coexist [R01-002, verified; R01-012, screened].

### Tags

`retrieval`, `fact`, `abstract`, `non-sensory`

## Method 12 — I write the subtraction in my head

**Strategy class:** algorithmic procedure
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + motor imagery
**Math:** `15 − 08 = 07`, hence `7`

### First-person account

> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I picture the written problem. I place 15 above 08, regroup one ten as ten ones, and see 15 ones minus 8 ones: 7. I may write 07 only to keep the columns aligned; the answer is seven.”

### Steps

1. Align `15` and `08` in a mental ones column.
2. Regroup one ten as 10 ones: 15 becomes 0 tens and 15 ones.
3. Subtract `15 − 8 = 7`; read aligned `07` as 7.

### Illustration brief

Use vector written work: `15 − 08`, then `0 tens + 15 ones`, then `15 − 8 = 7`. Avoid an unexplained negative ones column; all overlays must show exact regrouping.

### Mathematical note

The written algorithm converts one ten into ten ones, giving 15 ones minus 8 ones = 7 ones. The leading zero in `07` is notation, not extra quantity.

### Research note

This is a constructed report of imagined written work. Working-memory research shows verbal, visuospatial, and executive demands vary by strategy and format, but cannot identify this private imagery [R02-I04, verified; R02-I05, verified].

### Tags

`written-algorithm`, `regrouping`, `visual-symbolic`, `motor-imagery`

## Research notes

- The gallery separates computation from phenomenology. Literature supports overlapping strategy repertoires and changes in repertoire, execution, distribution, and selection; it does not license fixed “types” [R01-002, verified; R01-013, verified].
- Compensation and decomposition are exact transformations. Availability or execution should not become a universal recommendation or frequency claim [R01-010, verified].
- Finger use, finger gnosis, fine-motor skill, and neural activation are different constructs; associations do not establish necessity or causation [R02-F01, verified; R02-F02, verified; R02-F07, verified].
- Working-memory findings concern task demands, not direct windows into first-person imagery [R02-I01, verified; R02-I04, verified; R02-I05, verified].
- Retrieval should be described cautiously: fast responses can have multiple mechanisms, and “it appeared” is a constructed report, not a participant quotation [R01-012, screened; R01-002, verified].
