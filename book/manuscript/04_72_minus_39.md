# 72 − 39 = ?

## Opening spread

One subtraction can be a pile emptied, a gap measured, a round number repaired, or a written procedure. Every constructed account below reaches **33**.

> The first-person descriptions are constructed representative accounts, not quotations from research participants.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I trade a ten
**Strategy class:** standard algorithm with regrouping
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + verbal
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I write 72 over 39. I trade one ten for ten ones: now I have 6 tens and 12 ones. Twelve take away nine is three; six tens take away three tens is three tens. I have 33. I can point to each place-value change as I say it, so the written marks and the words stay aligned.”
### Steps
1. Represent 72 as 7 tens and 2 ones.
2. Exchange 1 ten for 10 ones: 6 tens and 12 ones.
3. Compute 12 − 9 = 3 and 6 − 3 = 3 tens.
4. Combine 3 tens and 3 ones: 33.
### Illustration brief
Vector/composite: show 7 rods + 2 unit squares, one rod exchanged for 10 squares, then remove exactly 3 rods + 9 squares. Label every quantity.
### Mathematical note
One ten equals ten ones, so 72 = 60 + 12 and subtracting 39 leaves 33.
### Research note
R01-014 supports varied subtraction and derived-fact strategies; this account is constructed.
### Tags
`algorithm`, `regrouping`, `place-value`, `visual-symbolic`

## Method 02 — I subtract forty and repair
**Strategy class:** compensation
**Phenomenology:** verbal + abstract
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “Thirty-nine is one less than forty. I do 72 − 40 = 32, then put the extra one back: 32 + 1 = 33. The repair is addition because I removed one too much when I used the friendly forty.”
### Steps
1. Write 39 = 40 − 1.
2. Compute 72 − 40 = 32.
3. Add back 1: 32 + 1 = 33.
### Illustration brief
Vector number line: −40 from 72 to 32, then +1 to 33; display `39 = 40 − 1`.
### Mathematical note
`72 − (40 − 1) = 32 + 1 = 33`.
### Research note
R01-010 reports jump and compensation strategies up to 100; no frequency is claimed.
### Tags
`compensation`, `benchmark`, `verbal`, `number-line`

## Method 03 — I measure the gap
**Strategy class:** difference as distance
**Phenomenology:** spatial + verbal
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I start at 39, not 72. I go to 40 (+1), then 70 (+30), then 72 (+2). The gap is 1 + 30 + 2 = 33. I keep the jumps separate so the distance is visible instead of treating the answer as a mysterious difference.”
### Steps
1. Start at 39 and jump +1 to 40.
2. Jump +30 to 70.
3. Jump +2 to 72.
4. Add 1 + 30 + 2 = 33.
### Illustration brief
Vector number line 39→40→70→72 with arrows +1,+30,+2 and a brace labeled 33. Caption “distance,” never “remove.”
### Mathematical note
`39 + 33 = 72`, so the difference `72 − 39` is 33.
### Research note

## Method 04 — I split the subtrahend
**Strategy class:** place-value decomposition
**Phenomenology:** verbal + symbolic
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I split 39 into 30 and 9. First 72 − 30 = 42. Then 42 − 9 = 33. Two small sentences keep the place values clear. I check that the two pieces still recombine to 39 before trusting the result.”
### Steps
1. Decompose 39 = 30 + 9.
2. Compute 72 − 30 = 42.
3. Compute 42 − 9 = 33.
### Illustration brief
Composite vector equation `72 − 30 = 42 → 42 − 9 = 33`; partition a 39 card into 30 and 9.
### Mathematical note

## Method 09 — I find the missing addend

**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I ask, ‘39 plus what makes 72?’ Add 30 to get 69, then 3 to get 72. The missing amount is 33, so 72 minus 39 is 33. Turning the subtraction around gives me a related addition equation that I can verify in one line.”
### Steps

2. Add 30: 39→69.
3. Add 3: 69→72.
4. Combine 30 + 3 = 33.
### Illustration brief
