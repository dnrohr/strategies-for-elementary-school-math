# 378 + 596 + 247 = ?

## Opening spread

Three addends can be kept in their given order, rearranged, decomposed by place value, or balanced by moving an amount from one addend to another. Every first-person account is a constructed representative account, not a research quotation.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I add in the given order
**Strategy class:** left-to-right accumulation
**Phenomenology:** verbal + symbolic
**Math:** `378+596=974; 974+247=1,221`

### First-person account
> “I start with 378 and 596. I make 974, hold it, then add 247. I keep the running total visible so I do not accidentally add one number twice.”

### Steps
1. Add `378+596=974`.
2. Add `974+247=1,221`.
3. Check the size against roughly 1,200.

### Illustration brief
Vector running-total line with 378, +596, =974, +247, =1,221; exact arrows and labels.

### Mathematical note
Sequential addition preserves the total; parentheses may be placed as `(378+596)+247`.

### Research note
R02-I04 and R02-I07 support cautious discussion of working-memory demands; this account is constructed.

### Tags
`left-to-right`, `running-total`, `verbal`

## Method 02 — I pair 596 and 247
**Strategy class:** associative regrouping
**Phenomenology:** abstract + visual-symbolic
**Math:** `378+(596+247)=378+843=1,221`

### First-person account
> “The second and third numbers look like a useful pair. I combine 596 and 247 to make 843, then add 378. The parentheses move, but the three quantities do not change.”

### Steps
1. Compute `596+247=843`.
2. Add `843+378=1,221`.
3. Compare with a 1,200 estimate.

### Illustration brief
Vector brackets group 596 and 247 first, then flow to 378; no ambiguous crossing arrows.

### Mathematical note
Associativity permits `378+(596+247)`.

### Research note
This is a constructed account; no claim that pairing is universally preferred.

### Tags
`associativity`, `regrouping`, `symbolic`

## Method 03 — I make a round hundred
**Strategy class:** compensation
**Phenomenology:** inner speech + moving numerals
**Math:** `596+4=600; 247−4=243; 378+600+243=1,221`

### First-person account
> “I move four from 247 to 596. One addend becomes the friendly 600, and the other becomes 243. The total stays put because what one addend gains, the other loses.”

### Steps
1. Transfer 4: `596+4=600`, `247−4=243`.
2. Add `378+600=978`.
3. Add `978+243=1,221`.

### Illustration brief
Composite vector tokens move from 247 to 596; show before/after and exact conservation equation.

### Mathematical note
`596+247=(596+4)+(247−4)=600+243`.

### Research note
R01-010 is verified for cautious discussion of compensation flexibility in a different number domain; do not generalize frequency here.

### Tags
`compensation`, `conservation`, `place-value`

## Method 04 — I split every addend by place
**Strategy class:** place-value decomposition
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + verbal
**Math:** `(300+500+200)+(70+90+40)+(8+6+7)=1,221`

### First-person account
> “I make three columns in my head: hundreds, tens, and ones. First the hundreds make 1,000, then the tens make 200, then the ones make 21. The columns are easier than carrying three whole numbers.”

### Steps
1. Add hundreds: `300+500+200=1,000`.
2. Add tens: `70+90+40=200`.
3. Add ones: `8+6+7=21`; total `1,221`.

### Illustration brief
Vector place-value columns with exact subtotal bands; show 21 regrouped as 2 tens and 1 one if desired.

### Mathematical note
Each addend is decomposed into equivalent place-value parts before recombination.

### Research note
R01-007/008 support separating conceptual and procedural knowledge; account is constructed.

### Tags
`place-value`, `decomposition`, `columns`

## Method 05 — I add hundreds, tens, and ones in a written column
**Strategy class:** standard algorithm
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + motor
**Math:** column sums `8+6+7=21`, `7+9+4+2=22`, `3+5+2+2=12` → `1,221`

### First-person account
> “I line up the three addends. I add the ones and write 1, carrying 2 tens; then the tens and carry; then the hundreds and carry. The alignment is doing part of the remembering for me.”

### Steps
1. Ones: 21, write 1 carry 2.
2. Tens: 7+9+4+2=22, write 2 carry 2.
3. Hundreds: 3+5+2+2=12; result 1,221.

### Illustration brief
Exact vector column addition with carries highlighted; no raster numerals.

### Mathematical note
The algorithm is place-value decomposition with regrouping.

### Research note
R01-007 and R01-008 support conceptual/procedural distinction; written procedure alone does not show understanding.

### Tags
`algorithmic`, `written`, `regrouping`

## Method 06 — I make a thousand first
**Strategy class:** benchmark decomposition
**Phenomenology:** abstract + spatial
**Math:** `378+596+247 = 378+(596+247) = 378+843 = (378+622)+(843−622) = 1,000+221 = 1,221`

### First-person account
> “I ask how much 378 needs to reach 1,000: 622. I take 596 and 247 together, which is 843, so after supplying 622, 221 remains. One thousand plus 221 is 1,221.”

### Steps
1. Compute `596+247=843`.
2. Subtract the 622 needed by 378: `843−622=221`.
3. Combine `1,000+221`.

### Illustration brief
Vector number-line bridge from 378 to 1,000, with 622 removed from the other subtotal and 221 remaining.

### Mathematical note
`378+843 = 1,000+(843−622)` because `378+622=1,000`.

### Research note
Constructed relational route; no empirical frequency claim.

### Tags
`benchmark`, `compensation`, `number-line`

## Method 07 — I rearrange to make friendly pairs
**Strategy class:** commutative regrouping
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + symbolic
**Math:** `378+247=625; 625+596=1,221`

### First-person account
> “I pair the 378 with 247 because their ones make 15 and their tens make 110. That gives 625. Then I add 596. I have not changed any quantity; I only changed the order.”

### Steps
1. Pair 378 and 247.
2. Add them to 625.
3. Add 596 for 1,221.

### Illustration brief
Three colored tiles rearrange so 378 and 247 touch; vector braces show pair then total.

### Mathematical note
Addition is commutative, so addends may be reordered.

### Research note

### Tags

## Method 10 — I use an abacus-like place map
**Strategy class:** place-value organization
**Phenomenology:** spatial + motor
**Math:** ones 21, tens 220, hundreds 1,000 → `1,221`

### First-person account
> “I put beads into three lanes: ones, tens, hundreds. The ones lane has 21, so I trade two groups of ten for 2 more tens. That makes 22 tens; I trade two groups of ten tens for 2 more hundreds, then trade 10 of the 12 hundreds for 1 thousand. What remains reads 1,221.”

### Steps
1. Gather the raw place sums: 10 hundreds, 20 tens, and 21 ones.
2. Trade 20 ones for 2 tens, then 20 of the resulting 22 tens for 2 hundreds.
3. Trade 10 of the resulting 12 hundreds for 1 thousand; read 1 thousand, 2 hundreds, 2 tens, and 1 one.

### Illustration brief
Vector three-lane bead board; show exact trades and final place labels.

### Mathematical note
The place map is a concrete organization of the same column sums.

### Research note
Constructed representation; no claim about abacus training or transfer.

### Tags
`abacus-like`, `spatial`, `motor`, `place-value`

## Method 11 — I move four without changing the total
**Strategy class:** conservation by redistribution
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + abstract
**Math:** `378+596+247=378+600+243=1,221`

### First-person account
> “I slide four units from 247 to 596. The two addends become 600 and 243, which are easier to combine. The total is conserved because the arrow has two ends: plus four here, minus four there.”

### Steps
1. Transfer 4 from 247 to 596.
2. Add `378+600=978`.
3. Add `243` for `1,221`.

### Illustration brief
Vector transfer arrow between addend tiles; before/after equations must match exactly.

### Mathematical note
For any `t`, `(a+t)+(b−t)=a+b`.

### Research note
R01-010 supports cautious discussion of compensation flexibility; evidence is not specific to this problem.

### Tags
`redistribution`, `compensation`, `conservation`
