# 3/4 of 20 = ?

## Opening spread

Try the problem first. Does “of” feel like sharing, scaling, or selecting? The answer is 15. The routes below change the unit, operation order, or representation. First-person accounts are constructed examples, not research quotations.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — Four equal groups, then three
**Strategy class:** equal partition + selection
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete
**Math:** `20 ÷ 4 × 3 = 5 × 3 = 15`

### First-person account
> A solver might say: “I deal out twenty counters into four equal trays. Five land in each. Three trays are the part I want, so I gather fifteen. I leave the fourth tray visible as the unselected quarter, which lets me check that all twenty counters were shared exactly once.”

### Steps
1. Partition 20 into 4 equal groups. 2. Count 5 per group. 3. Select 3 groups: `5 + 5 + 5 = 15`.

### Illustration brief
Vector trays with exactly five counters each; three selected, one muted; overlay only after count check.
### Mathematical note
One fourth is 5, so three fourths is three copies of 5.
### Research note
Constructed mathematics. Verified R03-014 supports a bounded common-unit framework, not a developmental sequence for this method.
### Tags
`equal-sharing`, `operator`, `visual-concrete`

## Method 02 — One fourth, then triple
**Strategy class:** unit-fraction decomposition
**Phenomenology:** verbal + symbolic
**Math:** `1/4 of 20 = 5; 3/4 of 20 = 3 × 5 = 15`
### First-person account
> “I hear three copies of one fourth. I solve the smaller question—one fourth of twenty is five—then three fives make fifteen. I can reverse-check the unit by multiplying five by four: that rebuilds the original twenty before I select three copies.”
### Steps
1. Rewrite `3/4` as `3 × 1/4`. 2. Compute `20 ÷ 4 = 5`. 3. Compute `3 × 5 = 15`.
### Illustration brief
Bar split into four equal sections; bracket one, then duplicate it three times; total remains 20.
### Mathematical note
The denominator names the equal unit; the numerator counts those units.
### Research note
Consistent with the unit-fraction framework in verified R03-014; phenomenology is constructed.
### Tags
`unit-fraction`, `decomposition`, `inner-speech`

## Method 03 — Divide, then multiply
**Strategy class:** operator algorithm
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic
**Math:** `(20 ÷ 4) × 3 = 15`
### First-person account
> “I see a two-step recipe: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top. The mental symbols change from 20 to 5 to 15. The first operation finds the size of one equal share; the second counts three of those same-sized shares.”
### Steps
1. Divide by 4. 2. Get 5. 3. Multiply by 3 to get 15.
### Illustration brief
Mental-workspace arrow `20 → ÷4 → 5 → ×3 → 15`; no competing quantities.
### Mathematical note
This creates the equal share before selecting three shares.
### Research note
“Fraction as operator” is used here as a mathematical description, not as a learner type or empirical classification.
### Tags
`operator`, `symbolic`, `procedural`

## Method 04 — Multiply, then divide
**Strategy class:** equivalent operator transformation
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal
**Math:** `(20 × 3) ÷ 4 = 60 ÷ 4 = 15`
### First-person account
> “I make three copies of twenty first—sixty—then spread that total across four equal shares. It feels like stretching the numerator. I check that dividing sixty by four gives an integer fifteen and that this matches the divide-first route exactly once.”
### Steps
1. `20 × 3 = 60`. 2. `60 ÷ 4 = 15`. 3. Check `15 × 4 = 60`.
### Illustration brief
Three equal 20-unit bars combine, then split into four equal 15-unit bars.
### Mathematical note
For this exact value, `(20 × 3)/4 = (20/4)×3`.
### Research note
Mathematical variant; no source establishes this as a typical choice.
### Tags
`equivalent-order`, `operator`, `abstract`

## Method 05 — A fraction bar
**Strategy class:** part–whole area model
**Phenomenology:** geometric + visual
**Math:** three of four equal regions represent 15
### First-person account
> “Twenty is one long bar. I cut it into four equal regions and shade three. Each region is five, so the shaded length is fifteen. Equal region widths matter: if one section were wider, the picture would no longer represent fourths of the same whole.”
### Steps
1. Draw a bar labeled 20. 2. Partition into four equal regions. 3. Shade three regions of 5. 4. Add to 15.
### Illustration brief
Vector bar with equal partitions and labels 5; use outline and texture as well as color.
### Mathematical note
The model shows the fraction of the whole and the value of each part.
### Research note
R03-005 supports a tested contrast between number-line and area training, not a universal ranking of models.
### Tags
`fraction-bar`, `part-whole`, `geometric`

## Method 06 — Four rows of five
**Strategy class:** array grouping
**Phenomenology:** visual-spatial
**Math:** `4 × 5 = 20; 3 × 5 = 15`
### First-person account
> “I lay out twenty dots as four rows of five. Three rows are three quarters. I can see fifteen without counting every dot. I still scan the row structure—three rows with five in each—to verify that the selected rectangle contains exactly fifteen.”
### Steps
1. Arrange 20 as 4 equal rows. 2. Count 5 per row. 3. Select 3 rows. 4. Count 15 selected dots.
### Illustration brief
Exact 4×5 array; three rows outlined, one muted; braces show five per row.
### Mathematical note
The array makes equal groups spatially parallel.
### Research note
Multiple representations are a design choice. Verified R03-006 and R03-014 support only bounded claims about coordinated fraction representations and tested interventions, not a single-format curriculum effect.
### Tags
`array`, `spatial`, `equal-groups`

## Method 07 — Twenty dollars
**Strategy class:** contextual scaling
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + verbal
**Math:** `3/4 of $20 = $15`
### First-person account
> “I imagine twenty dollars as four equal five-dollar shares. Taking three gives fifteen dollars. The context changes the objects, not the fraction. I keep the equal-share condition explicit, because four unequal piles of money would not provide a valid fourths model.”
### Steps
1. Represent $20 as four $5 shares. 2. Select three. 3. Add to $15.
### Illustration brief
Four labeled $5 tokens totaling $20; three selected; add labels in layout, not raster art.
### Mathematical note
Money supplies units but leaves the numerical operation unchanged.
### Research note
Illustrative context only; no claim that money is universally helpful.
### Tags
`context`, `money`, `concrete`

## Method 08 — Three groups of five
**Strategy class:** repeated addition
**Phenomenology:** rhythmic verbal + motor
**Math:** `5 + 5 + 5 = 15`
### First-person account
> “Once I know a quarter is five, I tap three times and say five, ten, fifteen. The taps mark groups, not twenty individual objects. I stop after the third beat and use a fourth imagined beat to confirm that four groups would return to twenty.”
### Steps
1. Find `20 ÷ 4 = 5`. 2. Add three 5s. 3. Stop at 15.
### Illustration brief
Three groups of five with three tap marks; fourth group visible but unselected.
### Mathematical note
Repeated addition is multiplication by the numerator after the unit is known.
### Research note
R03-014 supports unit-fraction composition; no source supports fixed “rhythmic learner” claims.
### Tags
`repeated-addition`, `motor`, `rhythm`

## Method 09 — A proportion
**Strategy class:** proportional reasoning
**Phenomenology:** relational + symbolic
**Math:** `x/20 = 3/4; 4x = 60; x = 15`
### First-person account
> “I match an unknown part of twenty to three parts out of four. Cross-multiplying gives sixty, and four equal shares of sixty leave fifteen. I read the proportion as equal ratios, then substitute fifteen to check that fifteen out of twenty reduces to three fourths.”
### Steps
1. Set `x/20 = 3/4`. 2. `4x = 3 × 20 = 60`. 3. `x = 15`.
### Illustration brief
Aligned schematic bars for 4 parts and 20 units, with equality sign and clear note that drawing is not to scale.
### Mathematical note
The equation preserves the ratio `3:4`.
### Research note
Constructed advanced route; do not imply a universal developmental order.
### Tags
`proportion`, `relational`, `symbolic`

## Method 10 — I recognize fifteen
**Strategy class:** direct retrieval + verification
**Phenomenology:** abstract + visual-symbolic
**Math:** `3/4 × 20 = 15`
### First-person account
> “Fifteen appears, perhaps because I have seen quarters of twenty. I check it afterward: four fives make twenty and three fives make fifteen. The quick arrival and the later justification are separate parts of my report; retrieval does not make the equality uncheckable.”
### Steps
1. Retrieve 15. 2. Reconstruct `20 ÷ 4 = 5`. 3. Verify `5 × 3 = 15`.
### Illustration brief
Minimal `3/4 of 20 → 15`, followed by a four-groups-of-five check; do not portray retrieval as fixed ability.
### Mathematical note
Retrieval is a memory route; the check supplies an explicit warrant.
### Research note
R03-004 supports associations among magnitude, division, and fraction arithmetic in its sample; not this phenomenology.
### Tags
`retrieval`, `abstract`, `verification`
