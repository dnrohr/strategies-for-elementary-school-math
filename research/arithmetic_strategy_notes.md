# Arithmetic strategy development

Working evidence record for task `R01`. This file is not reader-facing prose. Source IDs
refer to the canonical `research/source_log.csv`.

## Scope and search rationale

The search was organized around seven claims the book is likely to need: how counting
procedures change; when answers are retrieved or derived; how decomposition and
compensation are used; how multiplication strategies develop; how children select among
available strategies; and how conceptual and procedural knowledge interact. Searches
combined the operation or construct with `child`, `development`, `strategy choice`,
`longitudinal`, or `experiment`. Priority went to longitudinal and experimental studies,
choice/no-choice designs that separate preference from execution efficiency, and scholarly
work that complicates a simple counting-to-retrieval narrative.

Verification means the article text (or an author/institutional full-text copy) and its
bibliographic record were checked. Screened means the DOI and bibliographic details were
checked against Crossref plus an authoritative abstract or institutional record, but the full
article was not inspected; screened sources must be upgraded before supporting final prose.
No quotations below are intended for publication.

## Findings

### 1. Repertoires overlap; change has several dimensions

- In a longitudinal study of 88 children from first through third grade, children used a
  variety of informal strategies before formal instruction and continued to invent strategies
  after instruction. The study identified four broad levels of problem-solving development,
  but it does not warrant treating those levels as a universal age schedule. [R01-001,
  ERIC abstract; screened, pending full-text confirmation]
- Lemaire and Siegler separate strategic change into changes in repertoire, distribution,
  execution, and selection. Their 20-child longitudinal multiplication sample used multiple
  strategies within every testing session; speed and accuracy improved, distributions shifted,
  and choices became more adaptive. This is strong evidence against assigning each child one
  fixed strategy type. [R01-002, pp. 83–96, especially Results and General Discussion]

### 2. Counting, decomposition, and retrieval coexist

- Across Grades 1–4, Geary, Hoard, and Nugent coded trial-level use of retrieval,
  decomposition, and counting in 275 children. Retrieval and sophisticated counting were
  already present in Grade 1; decomposition increased over grades, and the rate of adoption
  varied with central-executive capacity even after several controls. The study supports
  overlapping trajectories, not replacement at a single threshold. [R01-003, PubMed abstract;
  screened, pending full-text confirmation]
- In Grades 3–4, higher working-memory span was associated with more frequent and faster
  retrieval on single-digit addition, especially as the smaller addend increased. This is an
  association, not proof that working-memory differences cause a child to retrieve facts.
  [R01-004, PubMed abstract; screened, pending full-text confirmation]
- Imbo and Vandierendonck found that 10- to 12-year-olds used retrieval, transformation, and
  counting, and that all three drew on executive working-memory resources in their dual-task,
  choice/no-choice design. Strategy selection and execution changed with age and individual
  characteristics. “Retrieval” should therefore not be pictured as literally effort-free.
  [R01-013, pp. 284–287 and 303–306]
- Thevenot and colleagues challenge the standard interpretation of very fast answers. In a
  response-time study of 42 ten-year-olds, several classic markers taken to indicate retrieval
  for very small non-tie additions were also consistent with fast counting. Their account does
  not establish that all apparent retrieval is counting, but it makes the mechanism an active
  dispute. [R01-012, publisher abstract and record; screened, pending full-text confirmation]

### 3. Derived facts depend on relations, task form, and prior competence

- Canobi, Reeve, and Pattison found that 6- to 8-year-olds' demonstrated understanding of
  additive composition, commutativity, and associativity was related to order-indifferent,
  decomposition, and retrieval strategies, as well as speed and accuracy. Because these are
  relations within one study, avoid turning them into a simple causal claim. [R01-005,
  PubMed abstract; screened, pending full-text confirmation]
- Dowker's individually calibrated task showed wide variation among 6- and 7-year-olds.
  Identity was easiest; inverse and complement relations were among the hardest. Children
  sometimes overextended addition relations to subtraction, and performance depended on
  the exact principle and presentation context. [R01-014, Methods and Discussion,
  especially paras. 3–16 and 44–60]
- In a three-week practice study, conceptually sequenced addition/subtraction problems
  improved transfer to unpracticed problems, while well-structured procedural practice also
  improved children's verbalized conceptual explanations. Initial procedural skill predicted
  conceptual advances. [R01-006, publisher abstract; screened, pending full-text confirmation]

### 4. Compensation is available before it is reliably adaptive

- Sixty third-graders at three achievement levels spontaneously used both jump and
  compensation strategies on two-digit addition and subtraction. In no-choice trials,
  compensation was as accurate and faster, but free choices did not consistently track either
  item characteristics or each child's measured efficiency. Possessing two strategies is not
  the same as selecting adaptively. [R01-010, abstract; pp. 581–588, Tables 2–4]
- In the earlier number-domain study, second-graders' adaptivity was defined against both
  task features and measured speed/accuracy of jump and split strategies. Higher-achieving
  children adjusted choices more than the other achievement groups. This supports
  person-by-problem definitions of adaptivity, while the narrow sample and instructed
  strategies limit generalization. [R01-009, publisher and institutional conference abstracts;
  screened, pending journal full-text confirmation]

### 5. Multiplication development includes changing models and procedures

- During a school year of multiplication learning, the children in Lemaire and Siegler's
  sample used retrieval, repeated addition, object-set counting, writing the problem, and
  “don't know.” Repeated addition increasingly used the larger multiplicand as the repeated
  unit and the smaller as the number of repetitions; retrieval became faster and more
  accurate. These are changes in execution as well as changes in frequency. [R01-002,
  pp. 87–93]
- Mulligan and Mitchelmore followed a small all-female sample four times during Grades 2–3.
  Twelve observed calculation strategies were grouped into direct counting, repeated
  addition, multiplicative operation, and (for division) repeated subtraction models. Use
  varied with age, numerical size, wording, and semantic structure. The inferred “intuitive
  models” should not be treated as direct phenomenological reports. [R01-011, institutional
  repository abstract; screened, pending full-text confirmation]

### 6. Conceptual and procedural knowledge interact bidirectionally

- With fourth- and fifth-graders learning mathematical equivalence, conceptual instruction
  supported generation and transfer of a correct procedure. Procedural instruction improved
  conceptual knowledge too, but transfer of the instructed procedure was limited. The study
  supports causal influence in both directions under different conditions, with stronger
  transfer following conceptual instruction in this task. [R01-007, abstract and General
  Discussion; pp. 175–189]
- Two decimal-fraction experiments with fifth- and sixth-graders support an iterative account:
  initial conceptual knowledge predicted procedural gains, procedural gains predicted later
  conceptual gains, and experimentally supporting correct problem representation improved
  procedural learning. Decimal fractions differ from elementary whole-number arithmetic, so
  the mechanism is relevant but not operation-specific proof. [R01-008, abstract and General
  Discussion; pp. 346–362]

## Interpretations for book development

These are editorial inferences, not direct empirical findings.

1. Organize galleries as repertoires available to a solver, not as a ladder of child “types.”
   Use age/grade only as approximate context.
2. Keep `strategy repertoire`, `strategy frequency`, `execution efficiency`, and `adaptive
   selection` separate in adult-facing notes. A child can know a method without preferring it,
   and prefer it without it being fastest on that item.
3. Treat “the answer just appeared” as a constructed report of access to an answer, not proof
   of a specific memory mechanism. Fast, unreported procedures remain plausible.
4. Present count-all, count-on, decomposition, compensation, and retrieval as potentially
   overlapping tools. Do not describe counting as inherently deficient or retrieval as the
   universal endpoint.
5. For multiplication, distinguish the mathematical model (equal groups, repeated addition,
   multiplicative relation) from the overt procedure and from subjective imagery.
6. Pair compensation with a “why it preserves the answer” relational diagram. Evidence that
   children can execute compensation does not establish that they understand or adaptively
   choose it.
7. “Conceptual” and “procedural” should not be used as opposites or stable learner traits.
   The studies support iterative interaction and imperfect measurement separation.

## Caveats

- Strategy identification often relies partly on verbal report, visible behavior, or response
  time. These measures do not provide transparent access to subjective experience.
- Studies differ in whether children choose freely, are required to use a named strategy, or
  receive a preceding fact. Frequencies are not comparable without the task context.
- Some central studies use small, culturally specific, or demographically narrow samples.
  Lemaire and Siegler report 20 children with complete longitudinal data; Mulligan and
  Mitchelmore's sample was female. Neither supports universal prevalence estimates.
- Accuracy and speed are common operational definitions of efficiency. They omit cognitive
  effort, explainability, transfer, confidence, accessibility, and a learner's purpose.
- “Advanced,” “sophisticated,” and “efficient” are authors' task-relative labels. The book
  should use descriptive strategy names and state the comparison criterion.
- Thevenot et al.'s counting account conflicts with a large retrieval literature. The correct
  editorial response is to label the mechanism disputed, not select one theory as settled.
- Findings from mathematical equivalence and decimal fractions illuminate knowledge
  interaction but should not be cited as direct evidence about a specific whole-number fact.
- Rows still marked `screened` require full-text verification before their claims enter final
  manuscript notes or the bibliography.

## Questions / leads

- Locate and verify full texts for R01-001, R01-003, R01-004, R01-005, R01-006, R01-009,
  R01-011, and R01-012 before chapter drafting uses them.
- Add a primary study directly comparing count-all, count-on-first, and count-on-larger with
  trial-level observation; Carpenter and Moser provide the longitudinal frame but a more
  focused source would improve method-level notes for Chapter 1.
- Find work that distinguishes spontaneous “make ten” from decomposition elicited by an
  experimenter or curriculum.
- Seek direct developmental evidence on multidigit multiplication decomposition and
  compensation, not only simple-fact multiplication or adult mental calculation.
- Decide which definition of adaptivity the book will use: fastest accurate method for the
  average child, fastest for that individual, or a broader purpose-sensitive definition.
- Check whether newer preregistered studies replicate the counting-model interpretation of
  apparently retrieved small additions.
- Research self-report validity separately before attaching phenomenological language to any
  computational-strategy result.
