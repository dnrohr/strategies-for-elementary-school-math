# HOW WE THINK ABOUT ARITHMETIC
## Production Specification for an Illustrated Book About Multiple Ways of Solving Elementary Mathematics

**Working title:** *How We Think About Arithmetic*
**Subtitle:** *Many Minds, One Problem*
**Document purpose:** This file is a production brief intended to be handed directly to Codex/Work or another capable agent to research, write, illustrate, lay out, and export the book.

---

# 1. Core concept

This book presents a sequence of elementary-school mathematics problems and, for each one, shows **as many genuinely distinct ways of solving and experiencing the problem as possible**.

The book is not primarily about teaching a single “best” arithmetic method. Its central subject is the diversity of mathematical thought.

Each chapter begins with a very simple problem, such as:

> **7 + 5 = ?**

The chapter then presents a large collection of plausible first-person accounts such as:

> “I start at seven and count five more: eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve.”

> “I see seven dots and five dots. I move three of the five over to complete ten, and then there are two left.”

> “I know five plus five is ten, and seven is two more than five, so the answer is twelve.”

> “I don’t really calculate it. Twelve just appears.”

These accounts should be paired with carefully chosen illustrations that make the strategy and the subjective representation understandable.

The book should repeatedly distinguish two questions:

1. **What computation or mathematical transformation did the solver perform?**
2. **What was the solver’s subjective representation of that computation?**

These are not the same thing.

For example, two people might both solve:

`11 × 12 = 10 × 12 + 1 × 12`

but one may experience this as:
- inner speech,
- written numerals,
- an array of blocks,
- rows of dots,
- a spatial movement,
- finger tracking,
- remembered multiplication-table language,
- or a largely non-sensory abstract relation.

The book should make that distinction one of its recurring intellectual themes.

---

# 2. Audience

The book should work simultaneously for:

- curious older elementary-school students,
- middle-school students,
- parents,
- teachers,
- mathematics educators,
- cognitive-science readers,
- adults interested in differences in mental imagery or thought,
- people interested in aphantasia, inner speech, number sense, visual thinking, or mathematical cognition.

It should **not** read like a conventional school workbook.

It should feel more like:
- an illustrated atlas,
- a natural-history book of mental strategies,
- a cognitive-science picture book,
- or a visual essay about how differently minds can represent the same mathematical object.

The mathematics should remain at an elementary-school level even when the commentary for adult readers becomes intellectually sophisticated.

---

# 3. Tone and style

## 3.1 General tone

The tone should be:
- intellectually serious,
- curious,
- concrete,
- nonjudgmental,
- visually inviting,
- precise without sounding academic for its own sake.

Avoid:
- “right-brained / left-brained” language,
- unsupported learning-style claims,
- implying that visual, verbal, or spatial thinkers are fixed categories,
- claiming a strategy is superior without evidence,
- presenting constructed first-person accounts as empirical quotations,
- patronizing language toward children.

The book should emphasize that:
- one person may use several strategies,
- strategy can change from one problem to the next,
- the same person may solve the same problem differently on different days,
- a solver may use one computational method but experience it in multiple sensory or symbolic forms,
- people may be unable to introspectively report every internal step.

## 3.2 Prose style

Prefer:
- short, vivid first-person descriptions,
- clear diagrams,
- restrained explanatory prose,
- occasional adult-facing sidebars with deeper cognitive interpretation.

Avoid long textbook exposition inside the main chapter flow.

The reader should be able to flip through the book visually and understand the essential differences among strategies.

---

# 4. Epistemic standard

This distinction is mandatory:

## 4.1 Constructed accounts

Unless an account comes from an actual interview or published source, it must be framed as a **constructed representative account**, not as a quotation from a real person.

Use language such as:

> “A solver might describe the experience this way:”

or, in a front-matter note:

> “The first-person descriptions in this book are constructed examples designed to make different plausible strategies vivid. They are not quotations from research participants unless explicitly identified as such.”

Do not fabricate demographics, ages, diagnoses, or survey results.

## 4.2 Research-supported claims

Claims about:
- child development,
- arithmetic strategy frequency,
- number sense,
- finger gnosis,
- inner speech,
- fact retrieval,
- number-line representation,
- decomposition,
- compensation,
- fraction understanding,
- visuospatial representation,
- procedural versus conceptual knowledge,

should be researched and cited.

Use primary literature, scholarly reviews, major mathematics-education sources, and reputable books where possible.

Create endnotes or chapter notes rather than cluttering the main visual pages.

# 5. Central conceptual framework

Every solution account should be analyzed on at least two axes.

## Axis A: Computational strategy

Possible categories include:

### Counting
- count-all
- count-on
- count-back
- skip counting
- repeated addition
- repeated subtraction

### Retrieval
- direct fact retrieval
- multiplication-table retrieval
- remembered pattern
- memorized algorithm

### Decomposition
- split one number
- split both numbers
- place-value decomposition
- make ten
- make one hundred
- factor decomposition
- distributive reasoning

### Compensation
- round up then subtract
- round down then add
- use nearby square
- use nearby benchmark product

### Relational reasoning
- inverse operation
- difference as distance
- equivalence transformation
- preserving a total while redistributing terms
- proportional reasoning
- fraction equivalence

### Algorithmic procedures
- standard column addition
- standard subtraction with regrouping
- long multiplication
- common-denominator algorithm
- standard fraction simplification

### Geometric reasoning
- arrays
- area model
- rectangle decomposition
- fraction bars
- number line
- tiling
- spatial partitioning

### Physical or embodied reasoning
- fingers
- tapping
- imagined movement
- grouping physical objects
- sharing
- hand motions
- gesture-based tracking

### Pattern-based reasoning
- ×11 pattern
- doubles
- near doubles
- powers of two
- arithmetic-sequence structure

---

## Axis B: Phenomenology / representational format

Possible categories include:

### Verbal / auditory
- full inner speech
- number words only
- rhythmic chant
- remembered teacher voice
- multiplication-table cadence

### Visual-symbolic
- printed numerals
- handwritten numerals
- moving digits
- written algorithm in a mental “workspace”
- color-coded symbols

### Visual-concrete
- dots
- blocks
- candies
- coins
- fingers
- rows of objects
- fraction bars

### Spatial
- number line
- distances
- jumps
- regions
- left-right movement
- upward/downward movement
- spatial arrangement of quantities

### Geometric
- area
- rectangles
- grids
- partitioned shapes

### Motor / embodied
- finger extension
- tapping
- hand movement
- imagined writing
- eye movement
- rhythmic body timing

### Abstract / non-sensory
- “just knowing”
- relational awareness
- transformation without imagery
- nonverbal sense of structure

### Mixed
Most real strategies may combine several modes.

The book should repeatedly show that:
**algorithm ≠ phenomenology**.

# 7. Chapter sequence

The chapters should appear in roughly chronological order for an elementary-school learner.

The sequence should reflect increasing mathematical structure, not merely increasing numerical size.

---

## PART I — NUMBER

### Chapter 1 — `7 + 5 = ?`

**Developmental role:** early addition.

**Why this problem is rich:**
- small enough for finger counting,
- large enough to cross ten,
- supports direct retrieval,
- supports “make ten,”
- supports doubles / near doubles,
- supports number-line movement,
- supports object grouping.

**Minimum strategy families to depict:**
1. count all 12 objects
2. count on from 7
3. count on from 5
4. fingers as counters
5. make ten: 7 + 3 + 2
6. use 5 + 5 + 2
7. use 7 + 7 − 2
8. direct retrieval
9. dot-pattern subitizing/chunking
10. number-line jumps
11. visual blocks
12. verbal counting
13. rhythmic tapping
14. abstract non-sensory answer retrieval

**Illustration opportunities:**
- child-sized hands,
- counters,
- colored dots,
- number-line hopping,
- blocks snapping into a group of ten,
- speech/thought bubbles,
- “mental blackboard” numerals.

---

### Chapter 2 — `15 − 8 = ?`

**Developmental role:** subtraction crossing ten.

**Key conceptual contrasts:**
- take away,
- count backward,
- count upward from 8,
- difference as distance,
- compensation,
- use of ten as an anchor.

**Strategies:**
1. remove eight objects from fifteen
2. count backward eight times
3. count up 8 → 10 → 15
4. 15 − 5 − 3
5. 15 − 10 + 2
6. think 8 + ? = 15
7. difference on number line
8. fingers as subtraction counters
9. direct retrieval
10. visual bar model
11. “ten and five” decomposition
12. imagined written subtraction

**Illustration emphasis:**
show that subtraction may be experienced as **removal** or as **distance**.

---

### Chapter 3 — `37 + 48 = ?`

**Developmental role:** two-digit addition and place value.

**Strategies:**
1. 30 + 40 + 7 + 8
2. 37 + 40 + 8
3. 37 + 50 − 2
4. 40 + 45
5. make 80 then add 5
6. standard column addition
7. base-ten blocks
8. money model
9. number-line jumps
10. visual tens rods and ones
11. mental written algorithm
12. verbal place-value decomposition
13. compensation
14. direct/partial retrieval of intermediate facts

**Important visual concept:**
show regrouping as both:
- a symbolic carry,
- and a physical conversion of ten ones into one ten.

---

### Chapter 4 — `72 − 39 = ?`

**Developmental role:** two-digit subtraction with regrouping.

**Why especially important:**
This may be one of the richest chapters because subtraction admits sharply different conceptual models.

**Strategies:**
1. standard borrowing/regrouping
2. 72 − 40 + 1
3. count from 39 up to 72
4. 72 − 30 − 9
5. 72 − 2 = 70; 70 − 37 = 33
6. difference on a number line
7. base-ten blocks
8. money/change
9. complement reasoning
10. bar model
11. imagined movement
12. verbal algorithm
13. written mental workspace

**Illustration opportunity:**
a paired spread:
- “I am taking 39 away from 72”
versus
- “I am measuring the gap between 39 and 72.”

These should look obviously different.

---

# PART II — GROUPS, MULTIPLICATION, DIVISION, SPACE

## Chapter 5 — `11 × 12 = ?`

This should remain a flagship chapter.

**Target breadth:** at least 18–25 distinct accounts.

Include:
1. direct fact retrieval
2. multiplication-table phrase
3. 10×12 + 12
4. 12×12 − 12
5. 11×10 + 11×2
6. repeated addition
7. skip counting
8. number-line jumps
9. array visualization
10. area model
11. grouped objects
12. ×11 digit trick
13. double/halve
14. factor regrouping
15. powers-of-two decomposition
16. long multiplication
17. finger tracking
18. tapping/rhythm
19. moving numerals
20. abstract non-sensory transformation

**Visual priority:**
This chapter should establish the book’s full visual vocabulary.

---

## Chapter 6 — `24 ÷ 6 = ?`

**Developmental role:** early division.

**Key distinction:**
division as:
- sharing,
- grouping,
- inverse multiplication.

**Strategies:**
1. share 24 objects into 6 groups
2. ask “how many sixes fit in 24?”
3. repeated subtraction
4. inverse fact: 6×4=24
5. skip counting by sixes
6. array partition
7. halve 24 then halve again where appropriate
8. fingers tracking group count
9. physical sharing
10. bar model

**Illustrations:**
hands distributing objects to six bowls or six children,
groups forming,
rows being partitioned.

---

## Chapter 7 — `7 × 9 rectangle: what is the area?`

**Developmental role:** multiplication becomes geometry.

**Strategies:**
1. recall 7×9
2. count unit squares
3. seven rows of nine
4. nine rows of seven
5. 7×10 − 7
6. 5×9 + 2×9
7. 7×5 + 7×4
8. tile rectangle
9. split physical space
10. standard multiplication without geometric imagery

**Core idea:**
contrast a solver who experiences this as “63” with one who experiences it as a literal rectangular region.

---

# PART III — FRACTIONS, PARTS, PROPORTIONS

## Chapter 8 — `3/4 of 20 = ?`

**Developmental role:** fraction as operator.

**Strategies:**
1. divide 20 into four equal groups, take three
2. find 1/4 then multiply by 3
3. 20 ÷ 4 × 3
4. 20 × 3 ÷ 4
5. visual fraction bar
6. array grouping
7. money analogy
8. repeated groups of 5
9. proportional reasoning
10. “three of four equal chunks”

**Illustrations:**
20 physical objects separated into four trays,
bar diagrams,
hands moving three of four groups.

---

## Chapter 9 — `Which is larger: 3/5 or 5/8?`

**Developmental role:** fraction magnitude.

**Strategies:**
1. fraction bars
2. number-line placement
3. compare each to 1/2
4. common denominator
5. cross multiplication
6. convert to decimals if developmentally appropriate as an optional later strategy
7. visual pie/area comparison
8. reasoning about missing amount to 1
9. benchmark fractions
10. proportional intuition

**Important note:**
Do not present circular “pizza” models as the only natural representation of fractions. Use bars and number lines heavily.

---

## Chapter 10 — `23 candies shared equally among 5 children`

**Developmental role:** remainder and transition from whole-number division to fractions.

**Possible outcomes:**
- 4 each, 3 left over
- 4 remainder 3
- 4 3/5 each if candies can be divided
- contextual objection: perhaps candies cannot be split

**Strategies:**
1. physical distribution
2. repeated subtraction of 5
3. 5×4=20, remainder 3
4. bar model
5. fraction continuation
6. grouping in fives
7. visual sharing
8. long-division precursor

**Core idea:**
the mathematics depends partly on what operations the real-world objects allow.

---

# PART IV — COORDINATING MULTIPLE TRANSFORMATIONS

## Chapter 11 — `27 × 46 = ?`

**Developmental role:** multi-digit multiplication.

**This should be another major flagship chapter.**

Possible strategies:
1. long multiplication
2. 27×40 + 27×6
3. 46×20 + 46×7
4. 30×46 − 3×46
5. 27×50 − 27×4
6. area model
7. lattice multiplication if historically/contextually appropriate
8. double/halve: 54×23
9. factor decomposition
10. partial products
11. repeated addition as a deliberately inefficient but conceptually valid route
12. calculator-like imagined column work
13. spatial blocks
14. verbal intermediate arithmetic
15. abstract decomposition

**Illustration opportunities:**
- large rectangle subdivided into 20/7 and 40/6,
- high-quality hands writing partial products,
- symbolic numerals “moving” into groups,
- side-by-side mental and written representations.

---

## Chapter 12 — `378 + 596 + 247 = ?`

**Developmental role:** coordinating several addends.

**Why important:**
introduces strategic ordering and conservation of total.

Strategies:
1. standard vertical addition
2. left-to-right running total
3. pair 596 + 247 first
4. 596 → 600 and compensate −4
5. move 4 from 247 to 596:
   - 596 + 4 = 600
   - 247 − 4 = 243
6. hundreds/tens/ones decomposition
7. group to make round numbers
8. estimate then refine
9. visual place-value blocks
10. mental abacus-like organization if represented carefully

**Core conceptual theme:**
quantities can be rearranged while preserving the total.

---

## Chapter 13 — `2/3 + 5/8 = ?`

**Developmental role:** adding fractions with unlike denominators.

**Core mathematical sequence:**
- recognize unlike units,
- construct a common unit,
- transform both fractions,
- add,
- interpret improper fraction,
- optionally convert to mixed number.

Strategies:
1. least common denominator 24
2. visual fraction strips subdivided to 24ths
3. area grid
4. multiples of 3 and 8
5. equivalent-fraction scaling
6. benchmark and estimate first
7. symbolic algorithm
8. convert to mixed interpretation after addition
9. number-line composition
10. proportional reasoning

**Visual requirement:**
the illustration must make clear that sixths/eighths/etc. are units of different sizes and cannot simply be added as raw counts.

---

## Chapter 14 — `3/4 + 2/3 + 5/12 = ?`

**Developmental role:** final elementary-school synthesis.

**Why suitable as a culminating chapter:**
the solver must coordinate:
- denominator structure,
- equivalence,
- ordering,
- multiple additions,
- simplification.

Strategies:
1. immediately convert all to twelfths
2. combine 3/4 + 5/12 first
3. combine 3/4 + 2/3 first
4. estimate relative to 2
5. visualize stacked fraction bars
6. number-line accumulation
7. symbolic common-denominator procedure
8. pair terms strategically
9. geometric area model

This chapter should emphasize **choice of intermediate representation**.

---

# 8. Standard chapter architecture

Each chapter should follow a recognizable but not rigid sequence.

## Opening spread
- Large problem statement.
- Minimal text.
- One striking illustration.
- Prompt to reader:

> “Before you turn the page, solve it. Then try to notice what happened in your mind.”

Optional prompts:
- Did you hear words?
- Did you see anything?
- Did you move your fingers?
- Did the answer appear before the explanation?

## Strategy gallery

Present approximately:
- 12–18 methods for simpler chapters,
- 18–25 for flagship chapters,
- 10–16 for fraction or geometry chapters where distinctions are broader rather than numerous.

Each strategy unit should contain:

### A. Short title
Examples:
- “I make ten.”
- “I count the gap.”
- “I borrow on paper.”
- “I see a rectangle.”
- “I hear the table.”
- “I move four from one number to another.”

### B. First-person account
Usually 40–120 words.

### C. Step sequence
3–6 concise steps.

### D. Illustration
Must depict the strategy sufficiently that a reader can understand it even if the prose is skimmed.

### E. Tags
For example:
- `decomposition`
- `visual-spatial`
- `inner speech`
- `finger tracking`
- `fact retrieval`
- `compensation`

### F. Optional “same math, different experience” callout
Use where two accounts share an algorithm but differ phenomenologically.

# 9. Visual design philosophy

The visual treatment is central to the book.

This must **not** look like:
- clip art,
- a worksheet,
- generic textbook graphics,
- a deck of SVG icons,
- AI images dropped in without mathematical checking.

The desired aesthetic is:
- sophisticated,
- warm,
- tactile,
- diagrammatically precise,
- editorial rather than classroom-commercial.

Think:
- modern illustrated nonfiction,
- information design,
- children’s science books,
- high-end educational publishing.

---

# 10. Hybrid illustration pipeline

Use different rendering methods depending on subject.

## 10.1 Use high-quality raster/generated illustration for organic subjects

Use generated or professionally styled raster illustration for:
- hands,
- fingers,
- faces,
- children/adults,
- bodies,
- gestures,
- tapping,
- imagined motion involving a body,
- physical objects such as candies, counters, coins, blocks, trays,
- scenes where a person manipulates objects,
- expressive cognitive scenes,
- perspective views.

The user explicitly does **not** want crude SVG hands.

### Hands are especially important

Hands must be:
- anatomically plausible,
- correct finger count,
- correct finger raised/lowered state,
- correct left/right orientation when relevant,
- posed in a way that actually corresponds to the arithmetic,
- free from fused fingers or extra digits,
- visually consistent across a sequence.

If generated imagery produces incorrect hands:
- regenerate,
- composite,
- retouch,
- or replace with drawn/stock-quality illustration.

Do not accept “close enough.”

### Example art prompt

> Editorial educational illustration, close-up of two human hands used to count addition, one hand showing five extended fingers and the second hand showing two extended fingers, anatomically correct hands, clean natural gesture, warm paper-like texture, modern children’s science-book illustration, restrained palette, soft dimensional shading, no text, uncluttered background, accurate finger count, no extra fingers, no fused digits.

### Gesture-sequence prompt

> Three-panel educational illustration showing the same pair of anatomically correct hands progressing through a counting strategy. Panel 1: seven represented across fingers; Panel 2: three more fingers conceptually moved/grouped to make ten; Panel 3: two remaining counters shown separately. Same hands, same camera angle, consistent style and proportions, no text, editorial nonfiction illustration.

---

## 10.2 Use vector/SVG/programmatic graphics for precise mathematical structures

Use SVG or programmatically generated vector diagrams for:
- number lines,
- fraction bars,
- arrays,
- area rectangles,
- grids,
- place-value blocks,
- equations,
- arrows,
- bracket annotations,
- decomposition trees,
- dot patterns,
- timelines,
- step labels,
- spatial relationships.

Reason:
these require mathematical precision and should not be entrusted to generative image models.

Vectors should be visually polished and integrated with the raster art.

---

## 10.3 Composite illustrations

Many pages should combine:
- a raster hand or person,
- vector arrows,
- exact numerals,
- number-line overlays,
- highlighted quantities,
- captions.

Example:
a child’s hand taps the table while a precise vector number line above the hand shows jumps of 12.

This hybrid approach is preferred.

# 11. Illustration language for mental experience

The book must visually distinguish internal representations.

## Inner speech
Use:
- subtle speech-like thought ribbons,
- translucent typography,
- auditory-wave motifs,
- repeated number words.

Avoid cartoon speech bubbles unless stylistically justified.

## Visual numerals
Use:
- large floating numerals,
- ghosted intermediate values,
- digits that separate or recombine,
- a faint mental “writing surface.”

## Motion
Use:
- directional arcs,
- trails,
- blur or repeated forms,
- movement arrows.

## Number line
Use:
- horizontal or gently curved space,
- physically meaningful jumps,
- strong endpoint emphasis.

## Rhythm
Use:
- repeated beats,
- hand-tapping illustration,
- waveform or pulse markers,
- synchronized numeric labels.

## Finger tracking
Use:
- realistic hand illustration,
- subtle numbering overlays,
- only as much vector labeling as needed.

## Arrays
Use:
- countable but uncluttered objects,
- visual grouping,
- braces or shaded regions.

## Abstract/non-sensory thought
Do not depict this as mystical light or a glowing brain.

Prefer:
- restrained relational diagrams,
- connected quantities,
- transformations without sensory metaphor,
- negative space.

## Memory retrieval
Depict:
- problem → minimal transition → answer,
- perhaps faint associative echo,
- without falsely implying a specific neural mechanism.

# 12. Color system

Use a restrained recurring palette.

Suggested semantic roles:

- **Blue:** starting quantity / stable structure
- **Orange:** adjustment / added quantity
- **Red:** removed quantity / correction
- **Green:** result / completed structure
- **Purple:** transformation / mental operation
- **Teal:** motion / spatial transition
- **Gray:** inactive or background structure

Do not rely on color alone.

All important distinctions must also be encoded by:
- outline,
- labels,
- position,
- pattern,
- shape.

Ensure color-blind accessibility.

# 14. Page composition

Prefer generous whitespace.

Typical strategy spread:

## Left page
- strategy title
- first-person description
- 3–5 step sequence
- tags

## Right page
- large illustration
- minimal caption
- perhaps a small “what changed?” overlay

For some methods, a full two-page visual spread may be better.

Avoid cramming multiple tiny methods onto one page if doing so sacrifices legibility.

# 15. Illustration quality control

Every visual must be checked against the mathematics.

For each illustration ask:

1. Does the depicted quantity match the text?
2. Are there exactly the stated number of objects?
3. Are group sizes correct?
4. Are number-line intervals correct?
5. Are fraction subdivisions equal?
6. Are fingers anatomically correct?
7. Are raised fingers consistent with the step?
8. Does arrow direction match the described mental operation?
9. Does color usage match the book’s semantic system?
10. Does the illustration accidentally imply a different strategy?

Mathematical correctness takes priority over aesthetic novelty.

# 18. Breadth requirements

The goal is not to generate superficial variants.

A new strategy counts as genuinely distinct if at least one of these changes:

- mathematical decomposition,
- conceptual model,
- sequence of operations,
- representational unit,
- direction of reasoning,
- memory mechanism,
- sensory format,
- spatial organization,
- embodied support.

Do **not** inflate method counts by trivial paraphrase.

For example:

> “I say 120 plus 12.”

and

> “I hear myself say 120 plus 12.”

are not separate computational strategies, but they might be useful as two phenomenological variants if explicitly presented as such.

# 19. Cross-classification feature

One of the book’s strongest recurring devices should be a matrix showing:

| Computation | Verbal | Visual-symbolic | Concrete | Spatial | Motor | Abstract |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

For selected strategies, intentionally show the same algorithm implemented in several different representational forms.

Example:

`11×12 → 120+12`

could appear as:
- inner speech,
- visual numerals,
- blocks,
- number-line motion,
- finger tracking,
- abstract relation.

This reinforces the book’s main thesis.

# 20. Recurring sidebars

Possible recurring sidebar types:

## “Same math, different mind”
Two people use the same algorithm but experience it differently.

## “Different math, same picture”
Two people use similar visual imagery but perform different operations.

## “Why this works”
A concise explanation of the underlying property:
- distributive property,
- associativity,
- compensation,
- equivalent fractions,
- inverse operations.

## “Try changing your mind”
Ask the reader to deliberately solve the same problem in another format.

Examples:
- solve without inner speech,
- solve without writing,
- solve using only a picture,
- solve using your fingers,
- solve by starting from the answer,
- solve using a number line,
- solve without visual imagery.

These should feel exploratory, not diagnostic.

# 21. Reader participation

At the beginning of each chapter, encourage readers to solve before seeing examples.

Possible small response prompts:
- “What appeared first?”
- “Did you hear a number?”
- “Did you see a shape?”
- “Did you move?”
- “Did you know the answer before you knew why?”

Optional blank reflection page at the end of each part.

# 22. Developmental chronology

The progression should feel natural:

### Early
counting and quantity

### Next
place value and regrouping

### Next
multiplicative groups

### Next
division and inverse relationships

### Next
geometry as multiplication

### Next
fractions and proportional parts

### Final
multi-step coordination

Do not claim that every child learns in exactly this sequence.

Frame grade/age associations as approximate.

---

# 23. Mathematical notation

Use elementary notation in main text.

Avoid unnecessary formalism.

Properties like distributivity may be named in sidebars, but the first explanation should be intuitive.

For example:

> “Twenty-seven groups of forty-six can be thought of as thirty groups, then removing three groups.”

Then optionally:

> This is an example of compensation and the distributive property.

---

# 24. Art style brief

Target aesthetic:

- editorial nonfiction
- restrained modern palette
- slight paper texture
- tactile but not cartoonish
- expressive hands and objects
- clean silhouettes
- subtle depth
- no glossy 3D classroom clip-art aesthetic
- no stock-photo look
- no uncanny photorealistic AI children

A lightly stylized illustration approach is preferable to photorealism.

People should be diverse across the book without turning diversity into a visual checklist.

Avoid tying cognitive style to visible identity.

---

# 25. Image generation workflow

For each organic illustration:

1. Write a precise art brief.
2. Specify exact quantities and gestures.
3. Generate image.
4. Inspect anatomy.
5. Inspect quantity.
6. Regenerate or edit if incorrect.
7. Composite precise mathematical overlays afterward.
8. Save prompt/brief alongside image for reproducibility.

Do not generate text inside raster illustrations.

All labels and numerals should be added during layout.

# 31. Acceptance criteria

The book is successful if:

1. A reader can understand at least 10 genuinely different ways to approach most problems.
2. Several chapters convincingly show that the same computational strategy can feel different internally.
3. Illustrations are attractive enough to function as a major reason to read the book.
4. Hands, fingers, and physical gestures look intentional and anatomically credible.
5. Mathematical diagrams are exact.
6. The book never confuses constructed introspective examples with empirical reports.
7. The progression feels appropriate to elementary mathematics.
8. Adult readers gain genuine insight without the material becoming too advanced for children.
9. The result feels like an illustrated cognitive atlas, not a textbook or worksheet.
10. Every chapter makes the reader want to solve the problem themselves and compare their own experience.

# 33. Possible future extension

A later volume could apply the same method to:
- algebra,
- geometry proofs,
- estimation,
- mental rotation,
- probability,
- early calculus intuitions.

But this volume should remain firmly centered on elementary-school mathematics.

The simplicity of the problems is a feature: it allows cognitive diversity to become visible without advanced mathematical knowledge obscuring the comparison.

# 34. Final creative direction

The book should leave the reader with a persistent realization:

> Two people can give the same answer, use the same mathematics, and still have had remarkably different experiences getting there.

The arithmetic problems are not merely exercises. They are probes into representation, memory, space, language, embodiment, abstraction, and strategy.

Treat every chapter as an attempt to make those invisible differences visible.
