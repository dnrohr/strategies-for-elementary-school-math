# 23 candies shared among 5 children

## Opening spread

What does “shared equally” permit? With indivisible candies, each child gets 4 and 3 remain. If candies may be broken into equal pieces, each child gets `4 3/5` candies. The quotient depends on the unit and the context. These first-person accounts are constructed examples, not quotations.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — Deal one candy at a time
**Strategy class:** equal sharing
**Phenomenology:** motor + visual-concrete
**Math:** `23 = 5×4 + 3`; quotient `4 R3`
### First-person account
> A solver might say: “I deal five candies around, again and again. Four rounds use twenty. Three candies are still in my hand, so everyone has four and three are left.”
### Steps
1. Deal one to each of 5 children four times. 2. Count 20 dealt. 3. Record 3 remaining. 4. State `4 R3`.
### Illustration brief
Five labeled bowls, four candies each, plus exactly three in a central remainder dish; verify every bowl matches.
### Mathematical note
`4 R3` is exact whole-object division with remainder 3.
### Research note
Equal sharing is a constructed scenario; R03-008 is screened qualitative evidence, not a universal developmental sequence.
### Tags
`sharing`, `remainder`, `concrete`

## Method 02 — Ask how many fives fit
**Strategy class:** grouping / repeated subtraction
**Phenomenology:** verbal + symbolic
**Math:** `23 − 5 − 5 − 5 − 5 = 3`, so 4 groups and remainder 3
### First-person account
> “I remove a group of five children’s shares four times. After twenty candies, only three remain—too few for another complete round.”
### Steps
1. Subtract 5 from 23 repeatedly. 2. Stop at 3 after 4 subtractions. 3. Count subtractions as quotient. 4. Record remainder.
### Illustration brief
Number-line jumps of −5 from 23 to 3, exactly four jumps; annotate “one group per jump.”
### Mathematical note
Each subtraction removes one equal round; the leftover is less than 5.
### Research note
R03-011 is screened and applies to earlier multiplication/division models; do not overgeneralize it.
### Tags
`repeated-subtraction`, `grouping`, `number-line`

## Method 03 — Build four complete groups
**Strategy class:** inverse multiplication
**Phenomenology:** relational + symbolic
**Math:** `5 × 4 = 20`; `23 − 20 = 3`
### First-person account
> “I know four groups of five use twenty. I compare twenty with twenty-three and see three left over. The quotient is four with remainder three.”
### Steps
1. Find largest whole `q` with `5q ≤ 23`. 2. `5 × 4 = 20`. 3. Subtract: `23 − 20 = 3`.
### Illustration brief
Four rows of five candies plus a remainder group of three; show multiplication and subtraction together.
### Mathematical note
Division asks for the largest complete equal grouping before the remainder.
### Research note
This is a mathematical inverse relation; any account of retrieval is constructed.
### Tags
`inverse`, `multiplication`, `remainder`

## Method 04 — Use a bar model
**Strategy class:** part–whole bar model
**Phenomenology:** visual-spatial
**Math:** `23 = 20 + 3 = 5×4 + 3`
### First-person account
> “I draw a 23-long bar. The first 20 is a clean rectangle made of four groups of five. A short tail of three cannot make another group.”
### Steps
1. Draw a bar of length 23. 2. Partition 20 into four 5-unit sections. 3. Leave a 3-unit tail. 4. Label `4 R3`.
### Illustration brief
Exact bar with four equal blocks of 5 and a distinct 3 tail; avoid implying the tail belongs to a child.
### Mathematical note
The bar makes quotient and remainder parts of one whole.
### Research note
Visual model is constructed; R03-009’s quotient subconstruct is screened.
### Tags
`bar-model`, `spatial`, `part-whole`

## Method 05 — Break the leftovers into fifths
**Strategy class:** fractional quotient
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + symbolic
**Math:** `23 ÷ 5 = 4 + 3/5 = 4 3/5`
### First-person account
> “After four whole candies each, I cut each of the three leftovers into five equal pieces. Each child receives one fifth from each leftover candy: three fifths more.”
### Steps
1. Give each child 4 whole candies. 2. There are 3 candies left. 3. Partition each leftover into 5 equal pieces. 4. Give each child 3 pieces: `4 3/5`.
### Illustration brief
Three candies subdivided into fifths, with one fifth from each sent to each of five bowls; exact 15 pieces.
### Mathematical note
When the material is divisible, the remainder becomes the fraction `3/5` of one candy.
### Research note
R03-008 and R03-009 are screened; the distinction here is mathematical/contextual, not a developmental claim.
### Tags
`fractional-quotient`, `sharing`, `partition`

## Method 06 — Count by fives on a line
**Strategy class:** skip counting
**Phenomenology:** rhythmic verbal + spatial
**Math:** `5, 10, 15, 20` gives 4 complete jumps; 3 remains
### First-person account
> “I chant the size of one complete round: five, ten, fifteen, twenty. That is four rounds. I stop before twenty-five because I only have twenty-three.”
### Steps
1. Start at 0. 2. Jump by 5 four times to 20. 3. Compare 23 with 20. 4. Record remainder 3.
### Illustration brief
Number line 0–25 with four equal jumps and a marked 23, visibly short of 25.
### Mathematical note
Skip counting tracks complete groups; the distance from 20 to 23 is the remainder.
### Research note
No source here supports a preferred rhythm or auditory mode; account is constructed.
### Tags
`skip-counting`, `number-line`, `rhythm`

## Method 07 — Long-division workspace
**Strategy class:** standard algorithm
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + motor
**Math:** `5 ⟌ 23 = 4 R3`
### First-person account
> “I write 5 outside and 23 inside. Five goes into two zero times, then into twenty-three four times. Four fives make twenty; three stays below.”
### Steps
1. Set up `23 ÷ 5`. 2. Write 4 because `4×5=20`. 3. Subtract 20. 4. Write remainder 3.
### Illustration brief
Vector written algorithm with arrows from 4×5 to 20 and remainder 3; numerals added in layout.
### Mathematical note
The algorithm records the same quotient–remainder decomposition as physical sharing.
### Research note
Procedural success should not be treated as complete conceptual understanding; verified R03-012 supports that bounded distinction for its fraction-arithmetic tasks and samples.
### Tags
`long-division`, `algorithm`, `written`

## Method 08 — Estimate before sharing
**Strategy class:** benchmark estimation + correction
**Phenomenology:** abstract + relational
**Math:** `25 ÷ 5 = 5`; 23 is 2 less, so quotient is 4 remainder 3
### First-person account
> “Twenty-five would give five each. We have two fewer candies, so one whole share disappears and three candies remain after four shares.”
### Steps
1. Use nearby multiple 25. 2. `25 ÷ 5 = 5`. 3. Subtract 2 to reach 23. 4. Recompute: `23 = 5×4 + 3`.
### Illustration brief
Two panels: complete 25-candy benchmark, then remove two and show 4 groups plus 3; exact counts required.
### Mathematical note
The estimate guides the search; the final equation verifies it.
### Research note
Compensation route is a constructed mathematical strategy, not a claim about typical children.
### Tags
`benchmark`, `compensation`, `estimation`

## Method 09 — Fairness check
**Strategy class:** relational equality
**Phenomenology:** verbal + social-concrete
**Math:** `4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 3 = 23`
### First-person account
> “I test a proposed fair share. Four each uses twenty, and the three leftovers cannot be given as whole candies without making someone different. So four each is fair, with three waiting.”
### Steps
1. Propose 4 per child. 2. Compute `5×4=20`. 3. Compare with 23. 4. Check leftover is 3 and less than 5.
### Illustration brief
Five equal bowls plus a separate remainder plate; fairness is shown by equal bowl counts, not by child identity.
### Mathematical note
Remainder form requires `0 ≤ r < divisor`; here `3 < 5`.
### Research note
The fairness framing is constructed; do not infer social-development claims.
### Tags
`fair-share`, `equality`, `remainder`

## Method 10 — Choose the answer format from the material
**Strategy class:** contextual interpretation
**Phenomenology:** metacognitive + mixed
**Math:** indivisible: `4 R3`; divisible: `4 3/5`
### First-person account
> “I ask whether a candy can be cut. If not, I say four each and three left. If yes, I can split those three among five and say four and three-fifths each.”
### Steps
1. Identify whether units are divisible. 2. Compute `23 = 5×4 + 3`. 3. Keep `4 R3` for whole candies. 4. Convert remainder to `3/5` if cutting is allowed.
### Illustration brief
Paired spread: whole candies with remainder plate versus three candies cut into fifths; never mix the scenarios.
### Mathematical note
The numerical quotient is the same decomposition; context determines whether the remainder is left over or repartitioned.
### Research note
Equal-sharing and fraction meanings are related but distinct; R03-008 is screened and pending full text.
### Tags
`context`, `remainder`, `fraction`
