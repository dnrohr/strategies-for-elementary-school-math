# Embodiment and finger use

Task: `R02`
Evidence review date: 2026-09-06

## Search rationale and scope

This note asks three narrower questions rather than treating “embodied arithmetic” as a single claim:

1. What do fingers or pointing do during counting and calculation?
2. Are finger sensorimotor measures associated with later arithmetic outcomes?
3. Do behavioral or neural associations establish a necessary or causal role for fingers?

The search prioritized peer-reviewed developmental studies, neurocognitive studies using finger-related regions of interest defined independently of the arithmetic task, and reviews that explicitly discuss conflicting results and educational implications. Records in `research/source_log.csv` use `verified` only when the article text (not only a search snippet or abstract) was checked; abstract-and-metadata checks are `screened`.

## Findings

### Fingers can provide a countable external structure

- In an experiment with 20 preschoolers, children counted chips more accurately when either they or a puppet pointed than when gesture was prohibited. Error patterns differed: self-produced pointing helped with both tracking objects and coordinating tags with spoken number words, whereas observed pointing chiefly supplied tracking information. This supports a concrete **offloading and coordination** account of gesture during object counting, not a general claim that movement improves all arithmetic. [R02-F06, abstract; screened]
- In Reeve and Humberstone's study of 65 children ages 5–7, profiles of non-motor finger gnosis, visuospatial working memory, and finger use/success in single-digit addition were associated. Finger-gnosis profile contributed to predicting finger-use/success profile beyond visuospatial working memory. This is evidence of association within a cross-sectional profile analysis; it does not establish that better finger gnosis causes better arithmetic. [R02-F05, Abstract; Discussion]

### Finger skills and numerical outcomes are associated, but effect size and direction matter

- Barrocas et al.'s critical review found that finger gnosis and fine-motor measures predicted several numerical outcomes across the reviewed developmental literature. The same review emphasizes that controlled contributions can be modest—for example, roughly 1–2% of variance in first-grade calculation in one cited study—and that longitudinal and correlational results do not identify the causal direction. [R02-F01, pp. 13–14, “Conclusion and Perspectives”]
- The 2023 scoping review identified 45 cognitive psychology/neuroscience studies and 29 mathematics-education studies, for 75 studies in total because one study appeared in both groups. It found that the two literatures often ask different questions and that only one included study directly connected finger sensorimotor skill, finger strategy, and arithmetic. Its authors call for critical appraisal and stronger studies before prescriptive educational guidance. [R02-F02, Abstract; “Discussion”; “Prospects for Future Research”]
- Earlier longitudinal reports are historically important leads: preschool neuropsychological measures predicted later arithmetic after controlling age/development in Fayol et al.; Noël reported that Grade 1 finger gnosis predicted a range of later numerical measures. Because only metadata and abstracts were directly checked here, use the review synthesis—not these rows alone—for reader-facing claims until full texts are verified. [R02-F03; R02-F04; screened]

### Finger-related activation is operation- and task-sensitive

- Berteletti and Booth defined somatosensory and motor finger regions of interest independently of their arithmetic data by using Neurosynth meta-analytic finger maps. In 8–13-year-olds, subtraction, but not multiplication, activated finger motor regions; larger subtraction problems produced more somatosensory activation, while better subtraction performance was associated with lower somatosensory activation. This pattern supports task-sensitive recruitment, not a claim that a child consciously imagines fingers whenever those regions are active. [R02-F07, Abstract; Methods; Results; Discussion]
- Across the reviewed literature, culture and learned counting routines vary. A finger configuration is therefore not a universal transparent code for number. [R02-F01, pp. 1, 13–14; R02-F02, Introduction and Discussion]

## Interpretations for the book

- A finger-counting spread can accurately show at least three functions that may coexist: one-to-one tags, a stable record of what has been counted, and a visible/motor representation of a quantity. Label these as functions of the depicted strategy, not properties of a “finger learner.”
- The book can contrast **using fingers now** with **having finger-linked representations recruited by a task**. Behavioral finger use, finger gnosis, and activation in finger-related cortex are different measurements and should not be collapsed into one phenomenon.
- Finger use is best presented as one available scaffold whose usefulness depends on task, strategy, age, experience, and how the fingers are structured. “Uses fingers” versus “does not use fingers” is too coarse for both research and illustration.
- For subtraction, a plausible illustration may show fingers as an external counter or tracking device. Neural evidence cannot determine that a solver experienced a conscious finger image, so any first-person phenomenology must remain a constructed example.

## Caveats

- Association and prediction are not causation. Earlier numerical skill may influence later finger skill, both may reflect another factor, or influence may be bidirectional. [R02-F01, pp. 13–14]
- Finger gnosis, fine-motor dexterity, observed finger use, and finger-counting proficiency are distinct constructs. Do not substitute one measure for another.
- The strength and even direction of observed relations can differ with age, arithmetic operation, problem size, strategy, and outcome measure. [R02-F01; R02-F02]
- Neuroimaging overlap does not by itself reveal a representation's content, prove necessity, or license a first-person report. [R02-F07]
- Small and selective samples constrain generalization: for example, R02-F05 studied 65 children ages 5–7, and R02-F07 studied children ages 8–13 in an fMRI paradigm.
- The evidence does not justify banning fingers, requiring them for every child, or claiming that finger counting is a universal developmental stage. Reviews explicitly describe unsettled mechanisms and limited causal evidence. [R02-F01; R02-F02]

## Questions / leads

- Which specific finger strategies (count-all, count-on, canonical configurations, part–whole decomposition) support which arithmetic outcomes?
- Does explicit finger-strategy instruction transfer beyond trained tasks, and for whom? Training claims require a separate risk-of-bias review before inclusion.
- How do blind children and cultures with different body-counting systems inform claims about universality and learned mappings?
- Can longitudinal work distinguish whether finger gnosis supports arithmetic, arithmetic practice sharpens finger gnosis, or both?
- For the book's art: which exact finger states can be depicted anatomically and mathematically correctly without implying that a configuration is culturally universal?
