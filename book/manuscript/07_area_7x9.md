# A 7 × 9 rectangle: what is the area?

## Opening spread

Before reading on, look at a rectangle 7 units tall and 9 units wide. What arrives first: 63, rows of squares, a remembered fact, or a region of space? The same area can be counted, partitioned, computed, or remembered. These are constructed representative accounts, not quotations from research participants.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I remember seven nines
**Strategy class:** retrieval
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “The answer is already there for me. Seven nines is sixty-three. I do not see nine rows or say every step; I recognize the multiplication fact and then check that the question asks for area.”

### Steps
1. Recognize dimensions 7 and 9.
2. Retrieve 7 × 9 = 63.
3. Name 63 square units.

### Illustration brief
Vector outline of a 7-by-9 grid reduced to a quiet silhouette, with `63` emphasized and “square units” outside. No object count is implied.

### Mathematical note
The area of a rectangle is the number of unit squares it contains: 7 × 9 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-002 supports overlapping multiplication strategies and strategy change; it does not establish that any particular solver retrieves this fact.

### Tags
`retrieval`, `abstract`, `multiplication-fact`

## Method 02 — I count the little squares
**Strategy class:** counting / unit enumeration
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I start in the top-left square and count every little square once. I keep going across a row, then continue on the next row. When the last square gets number 63, I know the area.”

### Steps
1. Partition the rectangle into 7 rows and 9 columns of unit squares.
2. Count each of the 63 squares exactly once.
3. Report 63 square units.

### Illustration brief
Precise vector 7-by-9 grid with a single path visiting all 63 cells once; mark only checkpoints 1, 9, 18, …, 63. Verify no extra cells.

### Mathematical note
Counting unit squares directly measures area; the count agrees with 7 groups of 9.

### Research note
Constructed account. Screened R01-011 may support only cautious discussion of model categories pending full-text verification.

### Tags
`counting`, `unit-squares`, `visual-concrete`

## Method 03 — I see seven rows of nine
**Strategy class:** equal groups / repeated addition
**Phenomenology:** visual-spatial + verbal

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I see seven horizontal rows. Each row has nine squares, so I say nine seven times: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63. The rows matter more than individual squares.”

### Steps
1. Identify 7 equal horizontal rows.
2. Treat each row as a group of 9.
3. Add 9 seven times to reach 63.

### Illustration brief
Vector grid with 7 rows in alternating blue/teal bands; overlay `9 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 9 + 9 = 63`.

### Mathematical note
Multiplication is compact repeated addition: 7 × 9 is seven groups of 9.

### Research note
Constructed account. Screened R01-011 describes repeated-addition models cautiously; it does not establish a developmental sequence for every learner.

### Tags
`repeated-addition`, `equal-groups`, `visual-spatial`, `inner-speech`

## Method 04 — I turn it and see nine rows of seven
**Strategy class:** commutativity / equal groups
**Phenomenology:** visual-spatial

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I rotate the rectangle in my mind. Now there are nine rows, each with seven squares. Nine groups of seven are easier for me to organize: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63.”

### Steps
1. Rotate the 7-by-9 rectangle without changing area.
2. Count 9 rows of 7.
3. Conclude 9 × 7 = 63 square units.

### Illustration brief
Two exact vector rectangles, 7-by-9 and rotated 9-by-7, linked by a rotation arrow. Both contain 63 cells.

### Mathematical note
The commutative property gives 7 × 9 = 9 × 7; rotation preserves the unit-square count.

### Research note
Constructed account. Screened R01-005 may inform cautious discussion of conceptual understanding, not a claim about this individual method.

### Tags
`commutativity`, `rotation`, `equal-groups`, `spatial`

## Method 05 — I make a ten and remove seven
**Strategy class:** compensation
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + spatial

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Nine is almost ten. I picture seven rows of ten, which makes 70. Then I remove the extra square at the end of each row—seven squares. Seventy minus seven leaves 63.”

### Steps
1. Extend the width from 9 to 10 squares.
2. Compute 7 × 10 = 70.
3. Remove 7 extra squares: 70 − 7 = 63.

### Illustration brief
Vector 7-by-10 grid with its rightmost column marked orange as exactly 7 excess cells; overlay `70 − 7`.

### Mathematical note
Because 9 = 10 − 1, 7 × 9 = 7 × (10 − 1) = 70 − 7 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-010 reports jump and compensation use in number tasks; it does not make compensation universal or superior.

### Tags
`compensation`, `place-value`, `area-model`, `visual-symbolic`

## Method 06 — I split seven into five and two
**Strategy class:** distributive decomposition
**Phenomenology:** visual-geometric

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I cut the rectangle after five rows. The top piece is 5 × 9 = 45, and the bottom piece is 2 × 9 = 18. I put their areas together: 45 + 18 = 63.”

### Steps
1. Partition 7 rows into 5 and 2.
2. Find 5 × 9 = 45 and 2 × 9 = 18.
3. Add the pieces: 45 + 18 = 63.

### Illustration brief
Exact 7-by-9 vector rectangle split after row 5; label regions `45` and `18`. Verify horizontal boundary and no missing cells.

### Mathematical note
(5 + 2) × 9 = 5 × 9 + 2 × 9 = 45 + 18 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-002 and R01-013 support strategy repertoires and working-memory demands broadly, not a fixed visual type.

### Tags
`decomposition`, `distributive-property`, `area-model`, `geometric`

## Method 07 — I split nine into five and four
**Strategy class:** distributive decomposition
**Phenomenology:** visual-geometric + motor

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I draw a vertical line after five columns. Seven times five is 35, and seven times four is 28. I can almost feel two tiles, then slide their totals together: 35 + 28 = 63.”

### Steps
1. Partition 9 columns into 5 and 4.
2. Compute 7 × 5 = 35 and 7 × 4 = 28.
3. Combine 35 + 28 to get 63.

### Illustration brief
Exact vector rectangle split into 5-column and 4-column regions labeled `35` and `28`; verify 7 rows in both.

### Mathematical note
7 × (5 + 4) = 7 × 5 + 7 × 4 = 35 + 28 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-002 supports strategy variation; tactile “tile” language is illustrative phenomenology.

### Tags
`decomposition`, `distributive-property`, `motor`, `geometric`

## Method 08 — I build it with tiles
**Strategy class:** physical tiling / unitization
**Phenomenology:** visual-concrete + motor

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I imagine laying square tiles. I make one row of nine, then place seven rows. The finished patch is a rectangle. The area feels like the covered surface: 63 tiles.”

### Steps
1. Lay out 9 unit tiles in one row.
2. Repeat the row 7 times with aligned edges.
3. Count 63 tiles and name the area.

### Illustration brief
Composite hand placing the final tile on a clean 7-by-9 patch; add vector row/column braces and `63 square units`. Raster layer has no text.

### Mathematical note
Tiling a region with non-overlapping unit squares gives its area: seven rows of nine contain 63 tiles.

### Research note
Constructed embodied account. Verified R02-F01 and R02-F02 support cautious associations between sensorimotor measures and arithmetic, not necessity of physical imagery.

### Tags
`tiling`, `unitization`, `embodied`, `visual-concrete`

## Method 09 — I walk the array with my eyes
**Strategy class:** skip counting / array traversal
**Phenomenology:** spatial + motor

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “My attention moves across the rows like a scanner. I jump nine squares at a time: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63. The movement gives me a route through the rectangle.”

### Steps
1. Start with the first row of 9.
2. Make six more jumps of 9, one per row.
3. Stop at 63 after seven rows.

### Illustration brief
Vector 7-by-9 grid with a teal path and six arrows between row totals; each jump is exactly 9 and endpoint is 63.

### Mathematical note
Skip-counting by 9 seven times is repeated addition and equals 7 × 9 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-002 describes repeated addition in a changing repertoire; R02-I07 supports caution about executive resources during arithmetic.

### Tags
`skip-counting`, `spatial`, `motor`, `repeated-addition`

## Method 10 — I make two rectangles and add their areas
**Strategy class:** area partition / additive composition
**Phenomenology:** geometric + abstract

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I do not see 63 little squares. I see a large rectangle made from a 7 × 5 rectangle and a 7 × 4 rectangle. Their areas are 35 and 28. The large region is their union, so 35 plus 28 is 63.”

### Steps
1. Partition the region into 7-by-5 and 7-by-4 rectangles.
2. Compute component areas 35 and 28.
3. Add non-overlapping areas to obtain 63.

### Illustration brief
Minimal vector area model with adjacent regions labeled `7 × 5 = 35` and `7 × 4 = 28`; verify no gap or overlap.

### Mathematical note
Areas of adjacent non-overlapping regions add: 7 × 9 = 7 × 5 + 7 × 4 = 63.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R01-007 and R01-008 support interaction between conceptual and procedural knowledge, not a specific subjective visual experience.

### Tags
`area-model`, `additive-composition`, `conceptual`, `geometric`

## Method 11 — I write the multiplication algorithm
**Strategy class:** written algorithm
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + inner speech

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “I write 7 × 9 on an imagined page and calculate 63. I may not picture a rectangle at all—the page of numerals is my workspace. The word ‘area’ tells me to attach square units.”

### Steps
1. Write the factors 7 and 9.
2. Multiply the single-digit factors: 7 × 9 = 63.
3. Attach square units because the quantity is area.

### Illustration brief
Vector mental-paper workspace showing `7 × 9 = 63` beside a faint exact rectangle; crisp numerals, no raster text, no unnecessary carry marks.

### Mathematical note
For single-digit dimensions, the multiplication procedure is direct; the context interprets the product as square units.

### Research note
Constructed account. Verified R02-I04 and R02-I05 support format-sensitive working-memory considerations, chiefly in adult experiments; do not generalize to every child.

### Tags
`algorithm`, `visual-symbolic`, `inner-speech`, `written`

## Method 12 — I know the product, then check the shape
**Strategy class:** retrieval plus inverse checking
**Phenomenology:** abstract + geometric

### First-person account
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Sixty-three comes to mind from 7 × 9. Then I check the picture: seven rows of nine should be a little less than seven tens, so 63 makes sense. The shape is a reason to trust the answer, not the route that produced it.”

### Steps
1. Retrieve 7 × 9 = 63.
2. Estimate with 7 × 10 = 70.
3. Confirm 63 square units fits the 7-by-9 rectangle.

### Illustration brief
Vector comparison of a 7-by-10 rectangle (70) and a 7-by-9 rectangle with one column removed (63); verify exactly 7 cells differ.

### Mathematical note
7 × 9 = 7 × 10 − 7 = 63, so the benchmark is an exact compensation check.

### Research note
Constructed checking account. Verified R01-010 supports flexible compensation in number tasks; it does not claim all retrieval is checked this way.

### Tags
`retrieval`, `estimation`, `compensation`, `geometric`
