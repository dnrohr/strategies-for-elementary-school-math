# 2/3 + 5/8 = ?

## Opening spread

Thirds and eighths name different-sized pieces. The accounts below are constructed representative examples, not quotations from research participants.

## Strategy gallery

## Method 01 — I make twenty-fourths
**Strategy class:** equivalent-fraction transformation
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + verbal
**Math:** `2/3 = 16/24`, `5/8 = 15/24`, so the answer is `31/24 = 1 7/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> A solver might describe the experience this way: “Twenty-four works because 3 × 8 is 24. I rename two thirds as sixteen twenty-fourths and five eighths as fifteen twenty-fourths. Now I add 16 and 15 pieces of the same size.”
### Steps
1. Use 24 as a common unit. 2. Multiply by `8/8` and `3/3`. 3. Add `16/24 + 15/24`.
### Illustration brief
Show strips partitioned into 3, 8, then 24 equal cells; label 16, 15, and 31 exact cells. Use pattern as well as color.
### Mathematical note
Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number preserves value.
### Research note
Constructed account; R03-014 and R03-013 are relevant verified anchors.
### Tags
`equivalence`, `common-unit`, `visual-symbolic`

## Method 02 — I count same-sized unit fractions
**Strategy class:** unit-fraction composition
**Phenomenology:** verbal + abstract
**Math:** `2/3 = 16/24`, `5/8 = 15/24`, total `31/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “Each third contains eight twenty-fourths, so two thirds is sixteen units. Each eighth contains three, so five eighths is fifteen. I hear a running count: sixteen plus fifteen, thirty-one. Because every counted piece is now the same size, the total is thirty-one twenty-fourths, or one and seven twenty-fourths.”
### Steps
1. Rename each fraction in `1/24` units. 2. Count 16 and 15 units. 3. Name 31 units as `1 7/24`.
### Illustration brief
Use one 24-cell strip with brackets for thirds and eighths and a “16 + 15” overlay.
### Mathematical note
Both addends count the same unit fraction, `1/24`.
### Research note
Constructed explanatory model; do not generalize the verified R03-014 intervention beyond its tested setting.
### Tags
`unit-fractions`, `equivalence`, `abstract`

## Method 03 — I use a 3-by-8 grid
**Strategy class:** area model
**Phenomenology:** geometric + visual-concrete
**Math:** 2 rows of 8 give 16 cells; 5 columns of 3 give 15; total `31/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “A 3-by-8 rectangle has 24 equal squares. Two rows show two thirds, and five columns show five eighths. I count the two marked collections separately, so the overlay is not accidentally counted twice: 16 plus 15 is 31. The grid supplies a common unit, not a union-area model.”
### Steps
1. Draw a 3×8 grid. 2. Mark two rows and five columns with distinct patterns. 3. Count 16 and 15 equal cells.
### Illustration brief
Create a precise 3×8 grid and a separate 31-cell result strip; use labels to prevent double-counting.
### Mathematical note
The 24 cells supply a common denominator.
### Research note
Constructed geometry; verified R03-006 and R03-014 support only bounded discussion of coordinated fraction representations.
### Tags
`area-model`, `common-unit`, `spatial`

## Method 04 — I scale each fraction without changing it
**Strategy class:** denominator scaling
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + motor
**Math:** `2/3 × 8/8 = 16/24`; `5/8 × 3/3 = 15/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I make a little stretch gesture: multiply top and bottom by eight, then by three. The fractions get finer names but keep the same size. Once both say twenty-fourths, I add. The paired numerator-and-denominator scaling preserves each value, producing 16/24 + 15/24 = 31/24.”
### Steps
1. Multiply `2/3` by `8/8`. 2. Multiply `5/8` by `3/3`. 3. Add over 24.
### Illustration brief
Show fraction cards expanding with paired `×8/×8` and `×3/×3` arrows; verify equal lengths before and after.
### Mathematical note
`8/8` and `3/3` each equal 1.
### Research note
Constructed procedural account; R03-011 cautions that correct procedure and conceptual knowledge can differ.
### Tags
`equivalent-fractions`, `procedure`, `motor`

## Method 05 — I place both fractions on a line
**Strategy class:** measurement / number-line composition
**Phenomenology:** spatial + visual
**Math:** `16/24 + 15/24 = 31/24 = 1 7/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “On a line divided into twenty-fourths, two thirds lands at 16/24. I move fifteen equal steps for five eighths. Eight of those steps cross the next whole, and seven remain. The endpoint is therefore 31/24, and every jump uses the same one-twenty-fourth interval.”
### Steps
1. Partition a 0–2 line into 24ths. 2. Mark 16/24. 3. Move 15 intervals to 31/24.
### Illustration brief
Draw proportional 24-part intervals, a blue start, fifteen orange jumps, and an exact endpoint label.
### Mathematical note
Equal intervals make addition a displacement in a shared unit.
### Research note
R03-005 supports bounded number-line intervention claims, not a fixed “number-line mind.”
### Tags
`number-line`, `magnitude`, `spatial`

## Method 06 — I estimate first
**Strategy class:** benchmark reasoning + equivalence
**Phenomenology:** abstract + verbal
**Math:** `2/3 ≈ .67`, `5/8 = .625`, exact `31/24 ≈ 1.292`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “The answer should be above one and below one and a half, near 1.3. I then compute 31/24. The estimate is a guardrail: it tells me whether the exact result is plausible, but it does not replace the common unit.”
### Steps
1. Estimate magnitudes. 2. Predict a result near 1.3. 3. Compute and compare `31/24`.
### Illustration brief
Show 1, 1¼, and 1½ benchmarks, then an exact 24-unit inset; distinguish estimate from answer.
### Mathematical note
Estimation checks scale, while equivalence establishes exactness.
### Research note
R03-004 supports an association between magnitude knowledge and fraction arithmetic; it is not causal evidence.
### Tags
`estimation`, `magnitude`, `self-check`

## Method 07 — I use a compact symbolic rule
**Strategy class:** symbolic transformation
**Phenomenology:** visual-symbolic + verbal
**Math:** `(2×8 + 5×3)/(3×8) = 31/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I multiply each numerator by the other denominator and put both products over the product of denominators. The top is 16 + 15, the bottom is 24. It is a compact way to rename both fractions, not permission to add denominators.”
### Steps
1. Form `(2×8 + 5×3)/(3×8)`. 2. Calculate 31/24. 3. Convert to `1 7/24`.
### Illustration brief
Show cross-multiplication arrows feeding a common 24-unit tray, with an explicit “not 3+8” warning.
### Mathematical note
The rule is derived from equivalent-fraction transformations.
### Research note
Constructed account; R03-011 is a verified conceptual/procedural caution.
### Tags
`algorithm`, `symbolic`, `common-denominator`

## Method 08 — I separate the whole from the remainder
**Strategy class:** improper-fraction interpretation
**Phenomenology:** relational + abstract
**Math:** `31/24 = 24/24 + 7/24 = 1 7/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “Once I have thirty-one twenty-fourths, I set aside twenty-four as one whole. Seven equal pieces are left. Saying one whole and seven twenty-fourths keeps the unit visible; ‘31’ alone would lose the meaning. Recombining 24/24 and 7/24 checks the improper fraction exactly.”
### Steps
1. Partition 31 units into 24 and 7. 2. Name 24/24 as one. 3. Read the remainder as 7/24.
### Illustration brief
Show a 31-cell strip with the first 24 bracketed as a whole and 7 highlighted as remainder.
### Mathematical note
Improper and mixed-number forms are equivalent descriptions.
### Research note
Constructed account; R03-013 supports caution about inferring understanding from a single procedure.
### Tags
`mixed-number`, `complement`, `relational`

## Method 09 — I use a decimal as a check
**Strategy class:** tool-mediated verification

### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I predict a result just over one, enter the expression, and see 1.291666… The decimal checks the scale, but it does not explain the exact fraction. I still derive 31/24 with twenty-fourths. The repeating display is approximate, while the common-unit fraction remains exact.”
### Steps
1. Estimate. 2. Evaluate the decimal. 3. Derive and compare the exact fraction.
### Illustration brief
Pair a layout-added calculator display with a 24-cell exact model; calculator is clearly labelled as verification.
### Mathematical note

### Research note
Constructed tool-use variant; no developmental claim.

## Method 10 — Same common-unit math, no picture
**Strategy class:** direct relational transformation
**Phenomenology:** abstract / non-sensory
**Math:** `2/3 + 5/8 = 16/24 + 15/24 = 31/24`.
### First-person account
**Constructed solver voice — not a participant quotation**
> “I do not notice a picture, spoken words, or imagined movement. I recognize twenty-four as a common unit, rename the fractions, and combine sixteen and fifteen. I can draw the steps afterward, but that diagram would explain the mathematics rather than reproduce my experience.”
### Steps
1. Recognize 24 as shared denominator. 2. Rename both addends. 3. Add and convert the improper fraction.
### Illustration brief

Add a caption outside the diagram: `An explanatory record, not a picture of an inner scene.` Do not show a head, brain, glow, sensory icons, moving digits, or a permanent “nonvisual thinker” identity.
### Mathematical note
The computation matches Method 01; only the reported format differs.
### Research note
Constructed account; R03-012 and R03-013 motivate modest claims.
